A book with years behind it, and people in every line of it.

What may be written here

Ten lines written here. Seven classes in the Act. They are not the same list.

A general insurance agency in Trinidad and Tobago writes inside Schedule 1, Type A of the Insurance Act, 2018, which prescribes seven classes of general insurance business. Our ten lines are products; these are the classes. Knowing which product sits in which class is not trivia — it is what a registration is granted for, and what a licence is granted for.

  • 1Liability Insurance Business

    Third-party liability, other than liability arising out of the use of a motor vehicle, a vessel or an aircraft, and other than an employer’s liability to its own workers. Public liability, products liability, professional indemnity, directors and officers.

    On the book
  • 2Marine, Aviation and Transport Business

    Vessels and aircraft and their machinery; goods on board; freight; third-party risks from their use; risks of building, repairing and docking a vessel; and transit risks by sea, inland water, land or air — so cargo and goods-in-transit live here, not in property.

    Written twice · none in force
  • 3Motor Vehicle Insurance Business

    Loss of or damage to a motor vehicle, and loss or damage arising out of its use, third-party risk included and transit risks excluded. Private, commercial and fleet.

    The bulk of it
  • 4Pecuniary Loss Insurance Business

    Loss from a debtor’s insolvency or failure to pay; loss from having to perform a guarantee you gave; and unforeseen expense. Business interruption is expressly excluded and sits in property. Bonds are here; so is credit and trade.

    Bonds only
  • 5Personal Accident Short-Term Insurance Business

    Accident and sickness cover, written only on the Act’s own terms: a year at most, benefits payable no longer than five years, no guaranteed renewability, and as a rider it may not exceed a quarter of the premium of the general policy it hangs on.

    Not on this book
  • 6Property Insurance Business

    Loss of or damage to real or personal property, and business interruption with it. Wider than it looks: household, commercial property and fire, machinery and plant, contract works, money and burglary are all one class under the Act, however many product lines they are on our shelf. Money and burglary have been written here and have nothing current on them.

    On the book, widely
  • 7Workers Compensation Insurance Business

    An employer’s liability to pay compensation to a worker, whether under statute or in negligence, for accident or injury related to the employment. Compulsory, and the wage declaration behind it is the part that gets missed.

    On the book
  • One policy, one class — usually. Where a policy covers risks in more than one class the Act lets the insurer put the whole of it in the class of the predominant risk, the rest riding as incidental business. Its own example: a home policy carrying legal liability is still classified property. It also permits splitting a policy across classes where each part is significant, priced separately and claimable separately.
  • Market practice is written into the schedule. The Act allows an insurer to classify by normal market practice as well as by the definitions — naming major petroleum risks and certain construction projects as business conventionally treated as marine even where the definition would read property — provided it is done consistently. That is a judgement, and judgement is what this seat is for.
  • Registration is per class and per type. A sales representative cannot be registered for a class the insurer, agency or brokerage behind them is not itself registered to write. So which classes you may advise on is a fact about the paperwork, not an opinion — and getting that fact straight for every client on this book is part of the job.
  • Cyber has no class of its own, which is exactly why it gets missed. A cyber policy is liability and pecuniary loss, with a property and business-interruption component depending on how it is written — three classes and a judgement, on an exposure every commercial client on this book now carries.

Quoted from Schedule 1 of the Insurance Act, 2018 (Act No. 4 of 2018), Type A — General Insurance Business, as published by the Central Bank of Trinidad and Tobago. Long-term business — life, annuities, health, pensions — is Type B of the same schedule and is a different registration; the branch writes it with a different team, and it is not this seat. A status above describes our own book as the record stood on 8 November 2026 — read off the cover terms, so “in force” means a term running now, not a line we once wrote — and not what the insurer is registered to write.

  • Guardian’s own PDFs fill themselves field by field from what we hold — the proposal, Know Your Policy, the due-diligence checklist — and attach themselves. Not a retyped copy: the insurer’s actual form, still editable at the other end.

  • The desk has already read the record and tells you what to raise first — the value carried unchanged for two terms running, the sum insured that no longer means anything, the premium paid late last year, the bank on the policy, the claim still open.

  • The client is reminded at fourteen days and again at ten, a call opens itself at seven, and when the insurer answers late the desk chases that too, on the record. Nothing lapses because a person was busy.

  • The client signs in with a code to their own inbox and reads it themselves — every year since inception, what it covered, what was billed and paid, and the claims — then confirms and signs on their phone.

  • It is pulled from all of them and scrubbed before you see it — duplicate terms, a mid-term change kept as its own policy, a vehicle carried under a changed number — and where the insurer’s figure and ours disagree, the desk says which and by how much.

Which is the difference, said in one line. A client here is not a transaction — a renewal keyed, a premium taken, a file closed until next year. A client here is a journey, and the system is built to carry it — what they hold, what they were told, what we did about it, and what happens next, in one place that every one of us can see. That is what this branch is actually good at, and it is what you would be given to work with.

To be honest about paper: it has not vanished. Contracts and cheques still come to us from the insurer, and a client still signs things. What does not exist here is a record that can only be found on paper — every document that arrives is logged, routed to the right department, signed for by name and tracked until it is answered, and the branch wrote that too. The work the cabinet used to make is gone. The judgement it was hiding is the job.

What the seat actually is

The part a system cannot do.

Everything below this line is the job. Everything in the next two sections is what we have already taken off your desk so that you can get to it. If you read this list and think that is what I do now, badly, between typing — that is exactly who this is for.

  • Endorsements and extensions

    The mid-term work, done properly: a vehicle on and a vehicle off, a sum insured moved, an interest noted, an extension written and the premium adjusted the right way round. Done late or done loosely, this is where cover quietly stops matching what the client believes they have.

  • The renewal conversation

    Between eleven and thirty policies come up every month. A renewal is not a notice forwarded; it is a conversation about what changed in the year, what the claims say, and whether the sums still mean anything. Right now that conversation mostly does not happen anywhere in this market.

  • Telling them plainly

    What the cover does, and what it does not. The average clause in words, not in a clause reference. The excess they have forgotten. We would rather a client hear an uncomfortable thing from us in January than a surprising one from a loss adjuster in June.

  • Compliance, driven rather than survived

    The Insurance Act binds an agent, an agency, a broker, a brokerage and a sales representative in one sentence, and the Central Bank’s market conduct guideline sits under it. One line matters more than any other here: nobody may cause a policyholder to discontinue or replace a policy without first discussing the advantages and the disadvantages. We do not wait to be told. Every endorsement, every extension and every renewal letter is written to it, and when the Act moves, the person in this seat is the one who notices and says so.

  • The claim, from their side

    You are not the adjuster. You are the person who makes sure the file was right before the loss and that somebody answers the phone after it. It is the only part of insurance a client ever describes to anybody else.

  • A record the next person can read

    What was advised, what was declined, what the client said and when. Not for an audit — for the ordinary reason that one day it will not be you opening that file.

Underwriting experience is required for this seat and we will ask about it in detail. Not because you will be accepting risk — the insurer does that — but because somebody who has sat on the other side of a submission knows what a surveyor’s report is worth, what a broker leaves out, and when a sum insured is a number somebody made up.

The part most people in this work never get

You will not be raising a ticket. You will be making the change.

If you have spent years saying this would work better if… to somebody who wrote it down and did nothing, read this part twice. Everything on this page was built in this branch, by people who were not hired as developers. So a change you ask for here is not a request that joins a queue at head office. It is a decision, written into the desk, running on every file that week. These are five we would expect you to want, and the branch is open to the ones we have not thought of.

  • A renewal that goes out early

    Thirty days ahead instead of three, with last year’s claims and what changed already on it. The timing is yours to set, per class if you want it that way — commercial earlier than private motor, because commercial takes a conversation.

  • An underinsurance flag that bites

    Declared value against the trend of the last five terms, and the average clause drawn to scale on the client’s own letter: on a hundred thousand dollar loss, this policy pays sixty-one. We already draw it. You decide when it fires and what it says.

  • A check before anything goes out

    An unoccupied property. A sum that has not moved in three years. A premium that does not agree with the cover and the claims in the term. The desk stops the file and names the check that stopped it, so nothing goes to a client on a Friday that somebody would have caught on a Monday.

  • What we want, where the agents can see it

    What we are keen on, what we put up politely high, what we would rather not place. On the advisors’ own screens, so they stop bringing you business that was never going anywhere.

  • The evidence that you were right

    What you put up, what you advised against, what renewed and what lapsed — by class and by month. Most people in this work never get to prove their judgement. Here it is a report that writes itself.

  • And the branch in front of it

    Twenty-one advisors and two managers who will bring you the business, and a branch that answers a query the same day. Your changes reach all of them the morning you make them.

To be plain about the division of labour: you are not expected to write code, and nobody will ask you to. You are expected to know what the change should be, to say it precisely, and to tell us when it is wrong once it is running. Somebody here builds it.

Before you spend fifteen minutes on this

This is not an eight-to-four job, and that is not about hours.

The hours are genuinely yours — that part is settled. What is not optional is that the work here is never finished being improved. Every month somebody asks what should change, and in this seat you are the one who answers. Read the two lists before you start, and be honest about which one you are on.

Probably not you
  • You want the day handed to you, the same way, every day.
  • You would rather be told the rule than be the one who decides it.
  • A change to the process reads to you as more work, not less.
  • You have stopped reading what the Act does, because nobody asks.
  • You are counting the years to the next step rather than doing anything about it.
Probably you

We are not asking you to work longer. Several people here do their best thinking at six in the morning and are gone by two, and that is fine. What we are asking is that the process is yours to argue with.
