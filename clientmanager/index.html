To be exact about the relationship: this is the branch’s own general insurance agency. The risk is carried by the insurer and the agency places with them; the underwriting authority is theirs. What sits with you is everything on the client’s side of that line — what we put up, how it is presented, the endorsement mid-term, the renewal conversation, the advice on what the cover does and does not do, and the compliance standard all of it is written to. The branch’s group health and group life book sits beside this one with its own team; it is not part of this seat.

What the seat actually is

The part a system cannot do.

Everything below this line is the job. Everything in the next two sections is what we have already taken off your desk so that you can get to it. If you read this list and think that is what I do now, badly, between typing — that is exactly who this is for.

  • Endorsements and extensions

    The mid-term work, done properly: a vehicle on and a vehicle off, a sum insured moved, an interest noted, an extension written and the premium adjusted the right way round. Done late or done loosely, this is where cover quietly stops matching what the client believes they have.

  • The renewal conversation

    Between eleven and thirty policies come up every month. A renewal is not a notice forwarded; it is a conversation about what changed in the year, what the claims say, and whether the sums still mean anything. Right now that conversation mostly does not happen anywhere in this market.

  • Telling them plainly

    What the cover does, and what it does not. The average clause in words, not in a clause reference. The excess they have forgotten. We would rather a client hear an uncomfortable thing from us in January than a surprising one from a loss adjuster in June.

  • Compliance, driven rather than survived

    The Insurance Act binds an agent, an agency, a broker, a brokerage and a sales representative in one sentence, and the Central Bank’s market conduct guideline sits under it. One line matters more than any other here: nobody may cause a policyholder to discontinue or replace a policy without first discussing the advantages and the disadvantages. We do not wait to be told. Every endorsement, every extension and every renewal letter is written to it, and when the Act moves, the person in this seat is the one who notices and says so.

  • The claim, from their side

    You are not the adjuster. You are the person who makes sure the file was right before the loss and that somebody answers the phone after it. It is the only part of insurance a client ever describes to anybody else.

  • A record the next person can read

    What was advised, what was declined, what the client said and when. Not for an audit — for the ordinary reason that one day it will not be you opening that file.

Underwriting experience is required for this seat and we will ask about it in detail. Not because you will be accepting risk — the insurer does that — but because somebody who has sat on the other side of a submission knows what a surveyor’s report is worth, what a broker leaves out, and when a sum insured is a number somebody made up.

Why this seat is not like the one you are in

Most general insurance jobs are ninety per cent re-keying.

This branch wrote its own software — thirty-nine files, about a hundred thousand lines, all in-branch. So the work that normally eats the day has already been taken off the desk. Here is exactly what, named, and every one of these is running today.

  • Last year’s file, before you open it

    Every term since the policy began: what was covered, at what value and premium, the change on the year before, what was billed, what was paid and how many days late, and the claims that fell in each term. You start reading at the risk, not at the filing.

  • The insurer’s notice, read for you

    Guardian General’s renewal notice is read on the device — period, premium, tax, every vehicle or location, every requirement — and each figure is checked against our own record. Where the two disagree, the desk says so instead of you finding out at claim.

  • Guardian’s own forms, filled exactly

    Not a retyped version: the actual PDFs. The private motor proposal, Know Your Policy, the Motor Quotation Request, the company due-diligence checklist, EB0010 and GIO001 — filled field by field from what we hold, signed on a phone, and still editable at the other end.

  • The claim is matched to the right term

    A loss from last March is assessed against last March’s sum insured and last March’s policy number, not against this year’s cover. A loss that falls in a gap between two terms is flagged as a gap, in words the client can answer.

  • New business starts already filled in

    The client signs in with a code to their own inbox and their Contact, their motor policies and their vehicles fill themselves in from the record. The permit is photographed on the phone. It reaches the quotes desk with the documents attached and a task already open. You are reading a submission, not assembling one.

  • The record is cleaned before you quote it

    Salesforce holds duplicate terms, mid-term changes kept as their own policy, and vehicles carried under a changed policy number. The desk merges them only on a real identity — a registration, a chassis, a serial — and names the record to correct rather than quietly fixing it.

None of this makes the work easy, and we are not going to pretend it does. What it does is give you back the hours that normally go on typing, so they can go on the risk, the cover and the client — which is the part you are actually good at and the part nobody is paying you for right now.

The part most people in this work never get

You will not be raising a ticket. You will be making the change.

If you have spent years saying this would work better if… to somebody who wrote it down and did nothing, read this part twice. Everything on this page was built in this branch, by people who were not hired as developers. So a change you ask for here is not a request that joins a queue at head office. It is a decision, written into the desk, running on every file that week. These are five we would expect you to want, and the branch is open to the ones we have not thought of.

  • A renewal that goes out early

    Thirty days ahead instead of three, with last year’s claims and what changed already on it. The timing is yours to set, per class if you want it that way — commercial earlier than private motor, because commercial takes a conversation.

  • An underinsurance flag that bites

    Declared value against the trend of the last five terms, and the average clause drawn to scale on the client’s own letter: on a hundred thousand dollar loss, this policy pays sixty-one. We already draw it. You decide when it fires and what it says.

  • A check before anything goes out

    An unoccupied property. A sum that has not moved in three years. A premium that does not agree with the cover and the claims in the term. The desk stops the file and names the check that stopped it, so nothing goes to a client on a Friday that somebody would have caught on a Monday.

  • What we want, where the agents can see it

    What we are keen on, what we put up politely high, what we would rather not place. On the advisors’ own screens, so they stop bringing you business that was never going anywhere.

  • The evidence that you were right

    What you put up, what you advised against, what renewed and what lapsed — by class and by month. Most people in this work never get to prove their judgement. Here it is a report that writes itself.

  • And the branch in front of it

    Twenty-one advisors and two managers who will bring you the business, and a branch that answers a query the same day. Your changes reach all of them the morning you make them.

To be plain about the division of labour: you are not expected to write code, and nobody will ask you to. You are expected to know what the change should be, to say it precisely, and to tell us when it is wrong once it is running. Somebody here builds it.

Why somebody moves

Four reasons to leave an agency, and what we do about each.

Most people in general insurance in this country are not unhappy with the work. They are unhappy with the conditions around it. These are the four we hear, and what is actually true here.

  • “I am measured by the clock”

    Here you are judged on the book. On the renewals that stay, the endorsements done properly, the clients who still answer the phone in three years. Not on when you arrived. Work the hours the work needs, where the work is best done — every tool on this page already runs on a phone, because it was built that way for the branch’s own agents.

  • “Nothing I suggest ever happens”

    A change you ask for here is built that week. Not a ticket, not a queue at head office, not a form that goes to somebody who leaves. The branch wrote its own software and the person who writes it sits in the same building. That is the whole offer, and it is the one nobody else in this market can make.

  • “There is nowhere above me”

    This agency is being built now. The person who runs the book from the start is the person who runs what it becomes — the team, the classes we add, the appetite. There is no queue of people ahead of you, because there is no queue yet.

  • “The systems fight me”

    Read the next two sections before you decide anything. Every one of them is running today and you can watch it work before you apply. If you have spent years doing by hand what a system should have done, that is the part of this page to read twice.

One thing we are not asking you for: your clients. Come for the work and the conditions, not because you can bring a book with you. We are not asking you to, we would rather you did not, and nothing on the application asks about it. Your own employer’s clients are your own employer’s.

Who we are looking for

Experienced, qualified, and out of room.

Somebody who has done this the old way and is tired of it. You have worked a general insurance book, you have sat on the underwriting side of a submission, and you are somewhere that has stopped growing, or stopped listening — where the only way up is waiting for somebody to retire, and every good idea you have had went into a note nobody read.

  • General insurance experience, required

    Years on a real book: renewals, endorsements, extensions, the claims that followed. Motor and property at minimum, because that is where this book lives. We would far rather real depth in two classes than a list of eight.

  • Underwriting experience, also required

    Time actually spent underwriting, not only administering around it. You will not be accepting risk here — the insurer does that — but you will be the person who knows what a submission is missing before it goes, and what a sum insured is worth when nobody has surveyed it in six years.

  • Papers, and where you are with them

    ACII, the CII Diploma or Certificate, CPCU, LOMA, a local qualification, or part-qualified and still sitting. Tell us exactly which and what is left. You will be the technical voice in the room with an insurer and a client, and it shows.

  • Judgement you can explain

    The application asks you about a risk you declined and one you wrote against the grain. Then it puts a renewal file in front of you and asks what you would act on first. That is the interview.

  • Ideas, and the nerve to say them

    We are open to being told we have got something wrong, and we change things the same week. That is worth nothing if the person in the seat has learned not to bother. If you have a list of things you would fix, bring the list.

  • Service as a standard

    This branch is known for answering. Somebody who leaves a client waiting three days is the wrong fit here, however good the technical work.
