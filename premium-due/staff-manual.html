Why this exists

The email chain was the filing system

Until now a premium in arrears lived in three places at once: a JotForm somebody submitted, a column in a spreadsheet somebody updated, and an email chain somebody remembered. When an agent moved, went on leave, or simply forgot, the history went with them. The policy lapsed and nobody could say what had been tried.

The engine replaces all three. Everything attaches to the policy. The client's survey answer, the agent's retention case, the manager's decision, and every note in between sit on one thread that anybody with the right access can read — today, or in eight months when the client calls back.

It is not extra admin on top of the work. It is the work, written down once instead of three times.

The accountability gate — agents

If you have cases past day 65 with no retention form, or 90-day lapses with nothing logged against them, you will not reach your dashboard until you have typed what you are going to do about them. It is not a formality — what you write is posted to those policies under your name, and your manager sees it.

If your book is clean you go straight in and the gate never appears. That is the point of it.

The clock

What is due, and when

A premium-paying policy runs on a 90-day clock from the missed premium. The engine stages every policy automatically — you never work this out yourself.

0–29Current
Nothing duePremium paying and up to date. These do not appear in your working list.
1–44Overdue
Agent Make contact and log what you find Most of these are a bank change, an expired card, or a payment not yet matched. One message usually settles it. Use the Overdue nudge template and write down what the client said.
45–74Survey window
Agent · Sales Support Send the 45-day client survey Three questions, thirty seconds, no login for the client. You cannot save a policy until you know why it is behind — hardship, a billing failure and dissatisfaction all need completely different answers. Send the link, or complete it with the client on the phone. If they don't answer, one reminder goes at about day 52 and then we stop asking until day 60.
60–89Retention
Client chooses · Agent files · Manager responds The 60-day notice goes out, and the case gets filed On day 60 the client gets a formal letter that quotes the day-45 questions and their own answers back to them, sets out exactly what happens on days 75, 88 and 90, and offers ten one-click options in four groups — settling the amount, affordability, questions about the policy itself, or speaking with us. The agent, their unit manager and the branch manager are all copied, so whichever the client picks, somebody is already across it. If the client says nothing, they are chased every 5 days. The agent files the retention case by day 65; the manager answers it within 5 days. Thirty days of runway instead of fifteen — and the manager is in it while their decision can still change the outcome.
90+Lapse / win-back
Agent · Sales Support Reinstatement, or document the loss Cover has ended. Reinstatement keeps the policy's original age and terms, so it is usually cheaper for the client than a new policy — but the longer it runs the more evidence the insurer wants. Recent lapses are live conversations; act on them first.
PendingNew business
Sales Support · Agent Chase the outstanding requirements Underwriting incomplete. The client is not covered yet and often does not realise it. This is also written API sitting in suspense — it counts for nobody until it completes.
One thing worth knowing

For a policy marked Premium Paying, the Days column in the portfolio is how long it has been on the books — not how far behind it is. The engine knows the difference and stages off the status flag instead. If you have ever seen a healthy policy show a four-figure day count, that is why, and you can ignore it.

Accountability

Both clocks, and why they exist

When a policy lapses, two explanations usually arrive at once: the agent let it go, and the manager never came back to me. Only one of them is ever true, and until now nobody could prove which.

Every case in the 60-day window has exactly one person holding it at any moment, and the engine says who. Open any case and the escalation ladder across the top shows four rungs — survey answered, client picked an option, retention case filed, manager responded — with the day count and, in plain words, who is being waited on.

ClockDeadlineIf it is missed
Client respondsAny time after the day-60 emailChased every 5 days automatically, with the manager asked to reach out. Stops the instant they pick an option.
Agent files the caseBy day 65The agent is emailed with their manager copied, and the case shows Waiting on [agent] to everyone who can see it. It appears on their sign-in gate.
Manager respondsWithin 5 days of filingThe manager is emailed every 3 days, with the branch manager copied from the second reminder. It appears on their sign-in gate.
Managers meet a gate too

An agent who files on time and then waits three weeks for an answer has been let down, not the other way round. So the gate cuts both ways: a manager with overdue responses does not reach their dashboard either until they have committed to clearing them, and that commitment is written to the case under their name — exactly as it is for agents.

That is the whole design. Neither side can quietly wait out the clock while the other carries the blame.

Read the flags before you judge

The branch view carries a Late — either side filter and a stat that splits the count into manager-side and agent-side. Look at the split before a unit meeting. A unit with six late cases where five are sitting on the manager's desk is not an agent performance problem.

Write it for the next person

"Called, no answer" tells nobody anything. "Called twice Tue/Thu, went to voicemail both times. WhatsApp delivered but unread. Trying her work number Monday — if nothing by 12 Sep this goes to lapse and I'll say so." tells your manager whether to step in, tells whoever covers your leave exactly where to pick up, and protects you if the policy lapses anyway.

WhatsApp opens with the message already written. Email opens your mail app with the subject and body filled in. Copy text puts it on your clipboard for anywhere else. Whichever you use, the message is written to the policy thread under your name — so the record shows not just that you made contact, but what you actually said.

Edit before you send. The template gets the facts and the deadline right; you know the client. A line that sounds like you will always beat a line that sounds like a system.

Do not send the same thing twice

Check the thread before you reach out. If the client answered the survey three days ago saying they'll pay at month end, a chase email will read as though nobody is listening — and that is how you lose a client who was already staying.

Flags

Duplicate and churn

The engine groups every policy by Client Number, which surfaces two patterns you would otherwise miss.

Duplicate flag — the same client holds more than one policy on the same plan code. Sometimes that is legitimate. Sometimes it is a replacement written over cover that was already in force, which costs the client and creates a conduct problem. Check before you assume.

Churn flag — the client has an active policy and a lapsed one. They have let cover go before. The active policy is more fragile than it looks, and it deserves attention before it follows the same path.

There is a third pattern the flags will show you: one client with several policies all overdue on the same day. That is almost never four problems. It is one failed bank mandate, and fixing the mandate fixes all four. Look at the client, not the policy.

By role

What is actually yours

Agent

You own the contact

  • Clear the gate honestly — write what you'll really do, not what sounds good
  • Work closest to lapse first. The list is already sorted that way
  • Get the survey out in the 45-day window. Don't wait until 74
  • File the 60-day case by day 65, with the fact find attached — without it your manager cannot decide, and the delay is recorded against you
  • Log every contact, including the ones that went nowhere
Unit Manager · ABM

You own the decision

  • Answer within 5 days of filing. Past that you are chased every 3 days and the BM is copied
  • Clear the Awaiting manager queue daily — a case sitting three days is three days closer to lapse
  • Reach out to the client yourself on the 60-day cases. You are copied on that email for a reason
  • Give an instruction and a deadline, not just approval
  • Watch for agents whose cases are always filed at day 88
  • Use the churn and duplicate flags in one-to-ones — they show selling behaviour, not just persistency
Sales Support

You own the flow

  • Route incoming surveys to the right agent the same day
  • Chase pending underwriting requirements — that is written API in suspense
  • Flag recent lapses for win-back while the window is still open
  • Spot the multi-policy clusters and tell the agent it is one mandate, not four cases
Branch Manager

You own the pattern

  • The scorecard shows who is working the funnel and who is letting it age
  • Premium in arrears is the number that matters — it is renewable income leaving
  • Repeated Allow lapse — documented from one unit is a conversation, not a statistic
  • The manager queue is the fastest read on whether the branch is responsive
  • Check the late split — manager-side versus agent-side — before you attribute a lapse to anyone

The point

Ninety days is not very long

A policy that lapses is not a number coming off a report. It is a family that thought they were covered and finds out they are not — usually at the worst possible moment. Everything the client paid in stops working for them, and where health has changed since they took it out, the cover they had may not be available again at any price.

Most of the policies in this system can still be saved. Almost all of them are lost for reasons that had nothing to do with the client wanting to stop: a bank mandate that failed, a card that expired, a bad month, a message that went to an inbox nobody reads.

Those are all fixable, and they are only fixable inside the window. That is the whole job.
