The cycle runs 45 / 60 / 75 / 90. At day 60 the client stops receiving letters and a manager picks up the phone; at day 88 the client is sent their own file — everything we did and did not do — and asked whether anyone from us ever reached them. This is what changes, what your clients will receive, and what each of us has to do differently.

Why the letters open the way they do

The research on lapse behaviour is consistent on one point: a loss reads about twice as strongly as the equivalent gain (Kahneman and Tversky, applied to policy lapse). So each letter opens with what is actually at stake for that client, in figures — not with what we need from them.

It also says the true thing rather than the same thing. A client paying two other policies is told this looks like a failed mandate. A client who has lapsed before is told they have been here before. A client with one policy is told what it pays and that it was priced on their health as it was then. Same process, three different opening paragraphs, because they are three different conversations.

No browser. No form. No password.

Every answer in every letter is a link that opens a reply already written for them — the policy number, the question and their answer all filled in. They press send. That is the whole interaction, and it works on any phone, on any network, without visiting a website.

The replies come to support@rickyrampersadbranch.com with sales support copied, the same addresses the Salesforce macro already uses. Once the engine is deployed the same buttons become one-tap and record instantly — but nothing has to wait for that.

Day 90 does not mean the same thing for every policy

Cover does not simply end if the policy has built a value. Under the contract's non-forfeiture terms the cost of cover can be met from that value, and an automatic premium loan may already be running against it. Telling that client their cover ends is not a hard truth — it is the wrong one, and it is a misstatement about their contract.

The letters now read Issue Date and APLamount from the portfolio and say the true thing:

  • Under about two years, no value — it lapses outright. Cover ends. Said plainly.
  • Has value — the cover can continue, paid for out of what the client already put in. Confirmed by the agent.
  • APL already running — named, with the loan amount. “You are not about to lose your cover this month. You are steadily spending what you have already paid in to keep it.”

That last one is the more motivating message anyway, and it has the advantage of being accurate. The manager is now required to state the non-forfeiture position in their response — it is one of the eight questions they answer by tapping, and their answer — not the engine’s inference — is what the client reads at day 88.

Nothing fires into a vacuum

Every sequence stops the moment it should: the client picks an option, the manager answers, or the premium is paid. Nobody gets chased for something they have already done.

Why these and not the old ones

Every answer is something we do, not something they admit. The old form asked whether the delay was intentional, what the reason was, and what steps they planned to take. All three ask a person to account for a failure before we have offered them anything — and 47 of 137 of them had never even been called.

Nobody is asked why they are late. The reason rarely changes what we can do, and asking makes it likelier they say nothing at all.

The evidence

Empathetic framing rather than demand framing raises voluntary payment by up to 20% (CFPB, on collections communications). A stated commitment — the second question — lifts response by about 5 percentage points over a reminder alone (embedded randomised retention trial). And a loss reads roughly twice as strongly as an equivalent gain (Kahneman and Tversky, applied to lapse), which is why the letter states what is at stake before it asks anything — and why one of the options is simply show me exactly what I’d be giving up.

Accountability

Both sides are on a clock now

When a policy lapses, two explanations usually arrive at once: the agent let it go, and the manager never came back to me. Only one of them is ever true.

ClockDeadlineIf it is missed
Client respondsAny time after day 45Reminded every 5 days. Stops on any answer.

Agent files the caseDay 65Emailed with the manager copied. Case reads Waiting on [agent]. Appears on their sign-in gate. Runs alongside the manager’s call, not before it.
Managers meet a gate too

An agent who files on time and then waits three weeks for an answer has been let down, not the other way round. So a manager with overdue responses does not reach their dashboard either until they commit to clearing them — and that commitment is written to the case under their name, exactly as it is for agents.

The manager queue is no longer only the cases an agent filed. It is every policy handed over at day 60 with nothing recorded against it. Waiting for the filing is exactly how a client ends up hearing from nobody: the agent is late, so the manager never sees it, so the manager is not late either, and the policy lapses with two clean records.

Before you attribute a lapse to anyone, look at the Late — either side filter. It splits the count into manager-side and agent-side. A unit with six late cases where five are sitting on a manager's desk is not an agent performance problem.

The point

Ninety days is not very long

A policy that lapses is not a number coming off a report. It is a family that thought they were covered and finds out they are not, usually at the worst possible moment. Everything they paid in stops working for them, and where health has changed since they took it out, the cover they had may not be available again at any price.

Almost none of these are lost because the client wanted to stop. They are lost to a bank mandate that failed, a card that expired, a bad month, a message that went to an inbox nobody reads.

All of those are fixable, and only inside the window. That is the whole job, and thirty days is a great deal more room to do it in than fifteen.
