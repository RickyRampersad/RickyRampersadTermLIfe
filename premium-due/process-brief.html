What the 152 responses told us

“Other” was the top answer to what clients planned to do — 68 of 133. The list was too short, so people fell through to free text nobody could count. Four options have been added to close that gap: already paid, need more time, reduce the premium, and explain what I am covered for.

Email is what they want — 98 of 132 chose it over text or phone. And 93% said the delay was not intentional. Almost nobody in this book is walking away on purpose.

The number that should bother us most

47 of 137 clients — better than one in three — said their agent had not contacted them at all about the overdue premium. Not that they missed the call. That nobody called.

Alongside it: Approval Status was blank on 78 of 106 cases agents filed for a manager decision. Three quarters got no answer.

Those two numbers are why this process changed. Not the templates, not the timing — the fact that on both sides, the step that was supposed to happen frequently did not, and nothing recorded it.

The point

Ninety days is not very long

A policy that lapses is not a number coming off a report. It is a family that thought they were covered and finds out they are not, usually at the worst possible moment. Everything they paid in stops working for them, and where health has changed since they took it out, the cover they had may not be available again at any price.

Almost none of these are lost because the client wanted to stop. They are lost to a bank mandate that failed, a card that expired, a bad month, a message that went to an inbox nobody reads.

All of those are fixable, and only inside the window. That is the whole job, and thirty days is a great deal more room to do it in than fifteen.
