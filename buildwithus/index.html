    The tools, named

    Everything on this list is live in the branch today.

    Not a roadmap, not a pilot. These are the things you would have your hands on in your first month.

    • Google Apps Script

      36 files and about 100,000 lines run the branch: the letters, the reminders, the boards, the claims, the renewals, the billing checks. Deployed as web apps on triggers. This is the single most useful thing you will learn here, and it is plain JavaScript.

    • Salesforce

      We read and write it from our own code — OAuth, SOQL, composite requests, tasks, Chatter posts. Not clicking through screens: querying it, reconciling it, and writing back what the record should say.

    • respond.io, on WhatsApp

      An agent on the branch's WhatsApp line answers staff questions about pending business — signed in, scoped, and never guessing. You would be the person who widens what it can answer.

    • The Claude API

      Used all over the branch to read a long history and draft a reply, with structured output and hard rules about what it may never say. You will learn to put guardrails on a model, which is a skill the market is paying for right now.

    • Microsoft Graph

      The branch mailbox sends and reads through Graph, with the application permissions to match. Every client letter the branch sends goes out this way.

    • pdf-lib and pdf.js

      Guardian's own forms are read in the browser and filled, field by field, from the record — so nobody retypes a policy number. Including a scanned form that needed its pages rotated to make it work.

    • Playwright and 175 test files

      It goes to clients, so it gets tested. You would write tests, and more than once a test you wrote will stop something wrong reaching a real family. That is the job.

    • Git, GitHub, GitHub Pages

      The website is the repository — 338 pages, nearly six hundred commits, and no build step. You commit, it is live. Your work is in the open with your name on it.

    Your first job, already waiting

    Two real jobs. Nobody has done them yet.

    We are not going to invent an exercise for you. Here are two things genuinely sitting undone in this branch right now. One of them is very likely your first fortnight.

    1. Nineteen forms that are still somebody else's

      The branch moved almost everything onto its own pages — quotes, claims, renewals, the document log — but nineteen staff forms are still on an outside form builder. Petty cash. The fuel log. Licence renewals. The visitors book. They do not talk to anything, nothing is filed where the branch can read it, and no report comes out of them.

      Four of those forms are the administrator's own. You would rebuild them as branch pages that file to a sheet, e-mail who needs to know, and report themselves. There are five or six finished examples in the repository to copy the shape from — that is the point of building them all the same way.

    2. 338 pages, and we cannot tell you which one is read most

      Every page on the branch site carries a view counter. There are 157 of them in place. Not one has ever recorded a single view, because the small script behind them was written and never deployed. So the branch publishes constantly and is honestly guessing about what anybody reads.

      This is a one-afternoon job with a permanent payoff. Deploy it, confirm it records, and then every Monday the branch knows what its clients and agents actually open. Small, finishable, and it changes a decision every week after.

    If you want to see what finishing one looks like, both are described in CLAUDE.md in the repository, in the branch's own words, including why neither has been done yet.

    How we choose

    Three gates. None of them is a CV sift.

    We do care where you have worked and what you were paid — the form asks both, plainly, so that the conversation can be honest. But none of that decides it. What decides it is what you do when something is badly designed.

    1. The form below — about fifteen minutes

      Where you have worked, what you changed there, what you are earning now, and three questions in your own words. It is longer than most application forms on purpose. If fifteen minutes is too long to spend on this, then it is honestly not the job — and we would both rather know that now.

      Write it yourself, plainly. Four honest lines about a spreadsheet you built beats a polished paragraph that says nothing.

    2. Then: one thing you would fix here

      You have already done the short version of this at the top of the page. If the form reads like you, we ask for the long one, and it is the whole interview. Look through what we have built, find something clumsy, missing, or that would save somebody an hour, and tell us about it — what is wrong, why it matters, and roughly how you would fix it.

      A page, a form, a process on this very site. You do not have to write the fix and you do not have to be right. We are reading for whether you can walk into an unfamiliar system, find a real problem and explain it to somebody else. That is the job, so that is the test.

      Send it however you like — an e-mail, a voice note, a page of writing. If you happen to use GitHub, you can post it there as an issue instead and we will see it. Most people do not, and it counts for nothing either way.

    3. Then a conversation, and a decision

      Twenty minutes with the Branch Manager about what you found and what you wrote. Terms and pay are discussed there, openly, against the figures you gave us. If it is not right for either of us, we say so and that is the end of it, with no hard feelings.

    Fair questions

    What people ask us.

    I am not a developer. Am I wasting my time?

    No. Nobody in this branch was hired as a developer and none of us would pass a developer's interview. But you do need to arrive having written something — an Apps Script, a formula that got away from you, a Zap, a page, something you got an AI to help you finish. That is the bar: low enough for somebody entirely self-taught, high enough that you are building in your first month instead of watching. What cannot be taught is the instinct for a bad process, and that is what we are really reading for.

    I have never touched Apps Script, or Salesforce.

    Neither had anybody here. They are learnable in weeks if you have already written anything at all, and you would be learning them on real work with AI beside you rather than on a course. What we would ask at the interview is not whether you know them — it is what you have built with whatever you did have.

    Is this really an administrator's job, or a developer's job with a cheaper title?

    It is genuinely an administrator's job. There is real admin every day and it has to be right, because an agent cannot work and a client waits if it is not. The difference from every other admin job is that the second half of your time is spent making the first half smaller, and you are given the tools and the authority to do that. If you only want to build, this is the wrong seat.

    Why is your code public? Is that not a risk?

    The website is served straight from the repository, so the pages are public by necessity. Client data is the opposite — it never goes near the code, it lives in a private sheet behind per-client tokens, and the rule is written into the repository's own notes. Keeping those two things apart is one of the first disciplines you would learn.

    I am employed. Will my current employer find out?

    No. The form goes to the Branch Manager's own address, not to a shared branch inbox, and he is the only person who reads it. We take that seriously because we ask for the same discretion from practising advisors on our other recruiting page.

    What about pay?

    Discussed openly on the call, once we both know the job is the right one. We will not print a figure here and we will not haggle at the end — you will hear it plainly before you ever have to decide anything.

    Where is it, and are the hours fixed?

    The branch is in Chaguanas, Central Trinidad, and this is a seat in the branch — the admin is physical as well as digital. Hours are the branch's working day; the specifics are part of the conversation.

    What if I do the GitHub issue and you say no?

    You will have read a real production codebase and written a clear technical issue about it, in public, with your name on it. That is a genuinely useful thing to have done either way, and we will tell you plainly why rather than leaving you guessing.
