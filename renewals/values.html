Motor and property settle by two different rules. Almost everyone applies the wrong one.

There is no average clause in the motor policy. There is one in HomeGuard. Getting these the wrong way round is how a renewal conversation goes wrong — and it is the single most useful thing on this page.

Motor — the lesser of two numbers

A total loss pays the market value at the time of loss, or your sum insured, whichever is lower. There is no pro-rata cut. It is a ceiling, applied twice.

Insured too low? You are capped at your own number. A vehicle worth TT$600,000 insured at TT$300,000 pays TT$300,000.

Insured too high? You collect the market value anyway. Every dollar of premium above market value buys nothing at all.

Section 2.4.1, GG-MOT-POL-APV-10/09

Property — the 85% average clause

If the sum insured is below 85% of the cost of rebuilding, you are your own insurer for the difference and every claim is cut by the same proportion — not just a total loss.

Insured too low? A TT$100,000 kitchen fire on a house insured at two-thirds of rebuild cost pays about TT$67,000.

Insured too high? Also wasted — settlement is capped at the sum insured but based on actual rebuilding cost.

Section 2.1(e), GG-FHO-POL-04/11

Which is why motor values come down and property values go up. A vehicle depreciates, so holding an old high figure is money burned on premium you can never claim. A building's rebuilding cost rises with materials and labour, so holding an old figure walks the client toward the 85% cliff.

Same instruction — review the figure every year — pointing in opposite directions.

The one exception worth selling

OEB 5, Agreed Value. On a total loss it pays the sum insured regardless of market value, less 15% a year pro-rata, less the excess. It converts the lesser-of rule into a known number — which is what an owner of a new or high-value vehicle actually wants.

It has one condition and it is routinely missed: a written valuation from an approved valuator at the start of each period of insurance. Without one the benefit silently reverts to market value. If a schedule shows OEB 5 and the file has no current valuation, the client is paying for something they will not receive.

Settlement figures are net of the excess. Agreed Value depreciates 15% a year pro rata across the current period of insurance, not across the age of the vehicle — the sum insured is re-set at each renewal from a fresh written valuation, so the clock restarts every year. Six months in, the agreed figure is 92.5% of the sum insured. Without a current valuation on file the benefit reverts to market value entirely.

What to say on the call. Not "your value is wrong". Say: "A total loss on this pays the lower of the market value and what you're insured for. Right now those two numbers are far apart, and the policy pays the smaller one. Let's agree the real figure so the cover matches the vehicle."

That is a five-minute conversation that either recovers premium or protects the client. Both are worth having.

What the local benchmarks imply

Across these Trinidad listings a vehicle holds roughly 85–90% of its value a year over the first five years, then flattens hard — a 2012 Fielder asks TT$62,000 and a 2018 asks TT$58,000, which is no real difference at all. Pickups hold value far better than saloons: a 2018 BT-50 asks TT$95,000 against a 2020 at TT$132,000 and a 2023 at TT$295,000.

Two consequences for the renewal call. On a vehicle under about six years old, stepping the value down 10% a year tracks this market and 15% cuts too deep. On anything past about eight years the curve is flat, so the annual reduction should stop — a client whose value keeps being cut on an old car is being quietly under-insured a little more every year.

The two questions that set a realistic figure

"What would it cost to build this house again today?"

Not what it is worth, not what it sold for. Square footage times current build cost per square foot, plus walls, fences, tanks, the pool, plus debris removal and professional fees. The land is not insured and never belongs in the figure.

"If everything inside were gone, what would replacing it cost — new?"

People under-state contents by half, because they price what they own second-hand. The policy pays replacement as new, so the sum insured has to be the new price of everything.

And the sub-limits, which no sum insured can fix. Unless an item is named on the schedule, contents pays at most 2% of the contents sum insured for any one valuable, 10% for all valuables in one event, and 5% for any one other item or piece of electronic equipment. Listing a ring or a laptop costs very little. Not listing it is the difference between a claim and a disappointment.

Salesforce

Yes — when a client agrees a new value, the field changes.

The value lives on Risk_Details__c and it is writable. The object already carries dedicated fields for recording an increase or a decrease and the date it takes effect, so a change leaves an audit trail rather than just overwriting history.

What to write

FieldTypeWhat goes in it
Cover1__cCurrencyThe agreed new sum insured. This is the field that matters.
Increase_Cover_1__cCurrencyThe amount of the increase, when the value goes up.
Decrease_Cover_1__cCurrencyThe amount of the reduction, when it comes down.
Increase_Effective_Date__cDateThe date the new figure takes effect.
Premium_Increase_01__cCurrencyThe premium effect of the change.
Depreciation_15_Option_2__cCurrencyThe Agreed Value figure after 15% a year, if OEB 5 applies.
Total_Cover__cDerivedDo not write this. It recalculates itself.

The one trap. Total_Cover__c and Total_Property_Cover__c are derived. A PATCH that includes them is rejected and takes the whole record with it. Send Cover1__c alone and Salesforce recalculates the totals correctly — this was tested against the live org, and the totals came back right.

Composite takes 200 records a call. Property values go to Cover1__c for the building and Cover_General_Contents__c, Electronic_Equipment__c, Stock__c and Plant_Equipment_Machinery__c for the rest.

Two data gaps worth closing first

No NCD is recorded anywhere

NCD__c is empty on every comprehensive motor risk in the current renewal year. Special Perils and Waiver of Excess are on all of them, so the discipline exists — the discount just is not being captured. You cannot tell a client "you are at 50%, protect it" from a blank field.

Make and year missing on some risks

Two vehicles carry a model but no make, because the make picklist has no BYD or MG entry — both now sell here. One risk has the year sitting in Age_of_Vehicle__c instead of Year_of_Manufacture__c, which makes every depreciation calculation on it wrong.
