Orphan Servicing Process

Ricky Rampersad Branch · Guardian Life of the Caribbean
Internal · draft

We ask orphan policyholders how they want to be looked after before we assign anyone to them. That inversion is the whole process. The client's answer decides the assignment rather than following it.

It is only possible because the data is clean. Almost every orphan policyholder on our books has a current e-mail address on file — far above what the wider database carries — so one send reaches nearly all of them, and the answers come back inside a week. That is years of the branch keeping records properly, finally spendable.

The second return is a record. Every filed review is evidence of what a client was told, what they understood, and how they felt they were treated. That is a market-conduct trail we have never been able to produce at this scale.

The short version

Verify the book. Check the money. Find the contract. Ask the client. File the review. Assign the household. Keep the promise. Seven stages, in that order, and the order is the point.

The unit of work is the household

We assign households, not policyholders. A household is its head — the Account record named SURNAME, FIRSTNAME HH — and everyone under it goes to one agent.

This is not new. The company already holds thousands of household accounts. What is new is that we use them: most currently carry a single member, so families sit in the system as unrelated strangers. We have found one address holding four clients on four separate household accounts — which under the old process is three agents calling one house in the same week, each knowing a quarter of the picture.

Three signals, and we use all three

  1. The household account — what the branch has already recorded.
  2. The address, normalised. Addresses are typed freehand, so the same house is rarely spelled the same way twice. Matching on house number and street name finds several times more shared addresses than matching the text exactly.
  3. A shared e-mail inbox — the cleanest of the three, because an inbox either matches or it does not.

Where they disagree, that is the record worth a person's attention. None of them is an automatic merge. People share an address without being one family, and a tenant reads the same as a spouse. Confirm before you merge.

Stage 1
Verify the book before anyone touches it

One afternoon of checking prevents most of what goes wrong later.

  • Contactability. Who has an e-mail, who has only a phone, who has neither.
  • Staff addresses on client records. A branch address in a client's e-mail field means that client has never received anything we sent.
  • Catch-all accounts. Some policies hang off a corporate account rather than a household. Any household logic built without excluding them produces nonsense.
  • Duplicate contacts. Same person, two records — they get written to twice. Match on e-mail.
  • Households. Reconcile the three signals above.
  • Blanks that matter. A client with no town cannot be routed to a territory.

What you may not rely on from the CRM: premium amounts and product names. Those come from policy administration.

Stage 2
Check the money before anyone is called

No orphan client is contacted about premium until the arrears have been checked against cash

An arrears figure in the CRM has two possible explanations and they need opposite responses. Either the client stopped paying — in which case it is conservation work — or the premium was paid and not applied, in which case telling a client they are behind, to someone holding receipts, does damage an apology will not undo.

We cannot tell which from the system. Someone checks the cash first.

Until that check is done, the opening contact verifies and listens. It does not chase, it does not quote a balance, and it does not ask why they stopped paying.

Everything else carries on. Stages 1, 3 and 5 run without touching the money question, so the check does not hold up the work — it holds up one sentence.

Stage 3
Find the contract

Before we ask an orphan client anything else, we establish whether they actually hold their policy document. Two fields answer it: one records the branch releasing the document, the other records the signed receipt coming back. The gap between them is the problem.

PositionMeaningWhat we do
Released, receipt returnedThe client has itNormal outreach
Released, no receiptWe do not know where it isSearch first, then write
Never releasedStill in the buildingWe can offer it today

Two rules that came out of this

  • Never tell a client we hold their document unless someone has physically seen it. For the never-released group that is safe. For the released-with-no-receipt group it is not — the document left with an agent, and where it is now is exactly what we do not know.
  • A missing receipt is not proof of non-delivery. Some clients have their document and the paperwork was simply never returned. So we ask; we do not assert.

Stage 4
Ask the client

One letter does not fit an orphan book. What we say depends on what Stage 3 found.

SegmentWhat the letter says
Has their documentYour agent has left, your policy is unaffected, tell us how you were looked after
Document unaccounted forWe have no receipt for your document — do you have it, and how shall we put it right?
Document still with usWe are holding it — how would you like to receive it?

The page asks; the e-mail tells

A client's own policy position — what they hold, what is paid to when — goes in the e-mail, addressed to their address of record, exactly as a premium notice would be. It never appears on a web page. The review page is public and unauthenticated by design, and a forwarded link must never expose somebody's policy.

Sending rules

  • One send per mailbox. Where two clients share an inbox, name both rather than sending twice.
  • Small segments first, in batches, with someone watching the replies.
  • A client with a phone and no e-mail gets a call, not a link.
  • Offer only what the branch can deliver every time. A second broken promise is worse than a narrower first one.

Stage 5
File the review

A conversation nobody files does not count. The filed review is the unit of work, because it is the only thing the system sees end to end — and it protects the agent as much as the client.

The client gives their name, date of birth and e-mail. That traces them against the portfolio automatically, so the agent picks up the phone already knowing what the client holds — without the client having to find a policy number.

None of what the trace finds goes back to the client's screen. It reaches the worklist, the agent brief and Customer Service. That boundary is deliberate.

Beyond the printed questions, the review asks whether they hold their document, whether anyone else in their home holds a policy with us, whether anything was left outstanding, and how they were actually treated. That last one is not a courtesy — a low score there is the most useful thing a client can tell us, and we do not defend the industry when it deserves the criticism.

The review produces form 2000-03-147 exactly as printed, with anything the shorter review did not ask named in the addendum — so a blank is never mistaken for a client who declined to answer.

Stage 6
Assign the household

Getting this wrong is easy and hard to undo. The original agent stays on the record. The new one goes in a different field.

FieldAction
Assigned AgentWrite the new servicing agent here. This is what the orphan process uses
Orphan AssignedSet to Yes
Date Assigned to AgentSet to the date of assignment
AGENTLeave untouched — it records who held the book
Writing AgentLeave untouched — it holds an agent code, not a name
Was Agent Paid for Orphan?Leave to whoever handles commission

This is not a new convention — every genuine reassignment in the system already works this way.

Assign whole households in one pass, so a partial run cannot split a family. And set the assignment date: it is barely maintained company-wide, and setting it consistently is what will let us measure a handover for the first time.

Stage 7
Keep the promise

What we tell an orphan client is simple: a review every six months and every birthday month, for life. It is on eight pages and in every film, so it has to be true.

All of it runs off one daily trigger, which also chases us when we miss our own deadline. If it stops, nothing fails loudly — the promise quietly stops being kept and nobody notices for months. That is why the branch wall shows a red banner across the bottom when it is off. If you ever see that banner, say so the same day.

The birthday month comes from the date of birth the client gives in their own review — their correction wins over whatever we held. One more reason the review has to be filed rather than simply had.

For an agent holding an orphan household: two contacts a year you do not have to think about, both arriving with a reason to call. That is the return on doing Stages 1 to 6 properly.

This is a draft. Argue with it.

Six things are still open — how many households per agent, who takes the heaviest arrears, whether we name the departing agent in the client letter, what we offer for document delivery, who checks the cash, and what counts as one review for the campaign number.
