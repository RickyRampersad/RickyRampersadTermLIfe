The Insurance Act · Trinidad and Tobago

How the law protects you

You do not have to take anybody's word for it — including ours. These are the protections written into the Insurance Act for every policyholder in Trinidad and Tobago, quoted as the Act states them, with what each one means in plain words.

If anyone suggests you replace or cancel this policy

A life policy cannot be transferred. No agent, no broker and no other company can move it. If anyone suggests you move, what is being proposed is that you end this one and buy a new one — and the Act sets out what they owe you before that can happen.

Before you cancel, and before you replace: they must go through the advantages and the disadvantages with you

Nobody may lead you to cancel a policy, or to replace a long-term one, without first sitting down and going through what you would lose as well as what you would gain. The Act names everyone this binds — agents, agencies, brokers, brokerages and sales representatives — and ties the discussion to a prescribed written form of disclosure.

The Act says
An agent, agency, broker, brokerage or a sales representative shall not (a) knowingly procure by fraudulent representations payment or the obligation for payment of any premium on an insurance policy; (b) cause a policyholder to discontinue an insurance policy without first discussing the advantages and disadvantages of the discontinuance of the policy; or (c) cause a policyholder to replace a long-term insurance policy without first discussing the advantages and disadvantages of the discontinuance of the policy, and shall comply with the standards on post-sale communication as prescribed in the Schedule and the form of disclosure set out in Guidelines.
And the standards it points to say
Any and all options presented post-sale to policyholders must include information that is adequate enough to enable policyholders to make informed decisions.
What to do: ask for it in writing, with both policies side by side — same sum assured, same term, same benefits, priced at the age you are now. “Set out for me what I gain and what I give up.” Anyone acting properly will put it in front of you without hesitation.

Nobody may talk you into cancelling, or into buying, with something untrue

Persuading you to cancel a policy, or to take out a new one, with a false or misleading statement — or by leaving out something you needed to know — is an offence. This one binds any person, not only people who sell insurance.

The Act says
A person who by (a) knowingly making a false, misleading or deceptive statement; (b) recklessly making, dishonestly or otherwise, any statement which is false, misleading or deceptive; or (c) dishonestly concealing material facts, induces or attempts to induce another person to enter into or cancel, or offer to enter into or cancel, any contract of insurance commits an offence.
What to do: if something you are told about your policy does not match what is in your hands, ask the branch to confirm it from your file. That takes one call.

Nobody may offer you a discount, a gift or a free month to win your business

The only premium that exists is the one written in the policy — for any company. An offer of a rebate, a cash gift or “I'll cover the first premium” to take out a policy is itself unlawful, and this binds insurers' staff, agents, brokers and sales representatives alike. It is also why this branch offers nothing to stay.

The Act says
No insurer and no officer, employee or agent of an insurer and no broker or sales representative shall directly or indirectly (a) make or attempt to make an agreement as to the premium to be paid for a policy other than as specified in the policy; or (b) pay, allow, give, offer or agree to pay, allow or give a rebate of the whole or part of the premiums stipulated by the policy or any other consideration or thing of value intended to be in the nature of a rebate of premium to any person who is insured or is applying for insurance in respect of life, person or property in Trinidad and Tobago.

Dividends and bonuses that the policy itself provides for are not rebates. They are yours under the policy.

Any comparison must be fair, complete, and based on verified facts

If a new policy is compared with the one you have, the comparison has to be balanced and cannot leave out anything that matters — including what you would give up.

The Act's standards of market conduct say
Comparisons or contrasts are based either on facts verified by the insurer or on reasonable assumptions stated within the promotional document. They should be presented in a clear, fair and balanced way and not omit anything material to the comparison or contrast. Material differences between the products must be set out clearly.

Whatever is recommended must be shown to be the most suitable thing for you

Before a product is put to you, your needs have to be understood and written down, and the recommendation has to be documented as the one that fits them — and that you can keep paying for.

The Act's standards of market conduct say
When assessing the suitability of a product or service for a consumer the insurer must at a minimum consider and document whether (a) the product or service meets that consumer's needs and objectives; (b) the consumer (i) is likely to be able to meet the financial commitment associated with the product on an ongoing basis …
What to do: ask what was written down about your needs, and when. A review of what you hold, done properly, is exactly that document — and it is the reason for the two doors at the foot of this page.

Only a registered person may sell or advise — and you may ask to see the certificate

Registration is tied to the company a person represents, and a person may be registered for only one insurer in a class of business at a time. When that contract ends, the Central Bank revokes the registration; anyone joining a new firm must hold a fresh certificate for that firm before doing any business for it. That is why the certificate is worth asking for: it tells you who the person is registered to act for, today.

The Act says
Every person registered under this Part shall produce his certificate of registration or a copy thereof when requested to do so by … an actual or a prospective consumer.
And
The Central Bank shall immediately revoke the registration of an agent, broker, brokerage, agency or sales representative where the Central Bank has … received notice … that the contract of an agent, broker, agency or sales representative has been terminated.
And
An agent, broker or sales representative that enters into a new contract with an agency, a brokerage or an insurer shall not conduct any insurance business unless he has received a certificate of registration …
And
An individual shall not be registered as a sales representative with more than one agency or insurer under the same class of insurance business at any particular time.
What to do: ask to see the certificate of registration, and read which company it names. The Central Bank keeps the public list of everyone who is registered, and will confirm a name on request.

What you told your insurer about your affairs stays confidential

Information about you and your policy, learned in the course of someone's work for an insurer, may not be passed on or used without your express consent.

The Act says
No registrant … and no director, officer, employee or agent of a registrant … who receives information in the course of his duties relating to the business or other affairs of a policyholder … shall disclose the information unless … the policyholder … expressly consents.

Anyone who can only sell one company's products must say so

So, in the spirit of the Act: this branch represents Guardian Life of the Caribbean and no other insurer. When we recommend something, it is a Guardian Life product, and you are entitled to weigh that. Whoever advises you — this branch included — is paid on the policy that is placed. So ask anyone which companies they can place you with, and what they would earn on a new one.

The Act's standards of market conduct say
Where an intermediary is tied to a single provider for a particular product or service the intermediary must disclose this fact in all promotional documents for the advertised product or service.

A broker with a special arrangement with an insurer must tell you so in writing

A broker arranges insurance on behalf of policyholders, and that is a fair thing to consider. If a brokerage holds an arrangement that lets it commit a particular insurer, or any arrangement unique to it, the Act requires it to disclose that to you in writing — so it is fair to ask any broker who recommends an insurer what arrangements they hold with it, the same question this branch answers about itself above.

The Act says
In the case of a brokerage that has entered into an agreement under which the brokerage can commit an insurer to provide coverage to a consumer in accordance with such criteria as may be established by the insurer, or an agreement that is unique, the brokerage shall disclose such arrangement to the consumer in writing in such manner as may be specified by the Central Bank.

What your policy already gives you

A policy that has been running for years has earned protections a new one does not have on its first day. These are the ones people give away without realising they owned them.

After three years, an honest mistake in the application cannot void it

Once a policy has run three years, the insurer cannot cancel it over an innocent error in the form you signed at the start. If this policy ever lapsed and was reinstated, the reinstated policy has the same protection. A brand-new policy, anywhere, starts that three-year clock again from the first day — on your health as it is now.

The Act says
A policy … shall not be void by reason only of any incorrect statement, other than a statement as to the age of the life insured, made in any proposal or other document on the faith of which the policy was issued or reinstated by the insurer, unless the statement (a) was fraudulently untrue; or (b) is material in relation to the risk of the insurer under the policy and was made within the period of three years immediately preceding the date on which the policy is sought to be voided …

A wrong age does not void it either

If your age was recorded wrongly when the policy was sold, the sum is adjusted. The policy stands.

The Act says
A policy is not void by reason only of a misstatement of the age of the life insured.

It does not lapse the day a premium is missed

If your policy has built a cash value that covers the premium, it stays in force. If not, the insurer must serve you a written late-payment notice and allow you twenty business days before it can be forfeited. Nothing happens silently.

The Act says
An ordinary policy shall not be forfeited by reason only of non-payment of any premiums where the surrender value of the policy, calculated as at the day immediately preceding that on which the premium falls due, exceeds the sum of the amount of the debts owing to the insurer under or secured by the policy and the amount of the overdue premium.
And where there is no such value
… unless on or after the day on which the premium fell due (a) the insurer liable under the policy serves a late-payment notice on the policyholder stating (i) the date of the notice, the due date of the premium and the amount due or payable to the insurer; and (ii) that the policy will be forfeited at the expiration of twenty business days after the date of the notice if the premium or a sum sufficient to keep the policy in force is not paid to the insurer within that period; and (b) a period of at least twenty business days has elapsed after service of the notice.
Worth knowing: that notice goes to your last known address — the Act says a notice posted there counts as served — so if you have moved, the one thing to do today is tell the branch the new address. And on a participating policy, bonuses you have already earned may be used to keep the policy in force; the branch will tell you if yours qualifies.

After three years of premiums, you have a third option: a paid-up policy

You are never limited to “keep paying” or “cash it in”. After three years of premiums you may stop paying and keep a smaller policy, fully paid, for life. And if you do choose to surrender, the cash surrender value is yours by right.

The Act says
A policyholder who desires to discontinue further premium payments on a policy on which not less than three years' premiums have been paid in cash shall, where the policy has a cash surrender value, be entitled on application to the insurer to receive in lieu of the cash surrender value a paid-up policy.
And
… shall on application to the insurer be entitled to surrender the policy and to receive not less than the cash surrender value of the policy less the amount of any debt owing to the insurer under or secured by the policy and less any applicable surrender penalties.
What to do: before anyone talks about cashing in a policy, ask for three figures in writing — what you have paid in, what a surrender would pay today, and what the paid-up value is. The gap between them is the real cost of starting over, and the paid-up figure is the option most people are never offered.

A savings annuity under five years old cannot simply be cashed in to move it

If what you hold is an annuity still in its saving stage and it is less than five years old, the Act allows it to be surrendered only where you need the cash in genuine financial hardship. A proposal to “transfer” it elsewhere is proposing something the Act restricts.

The Act says
The owner of an annuity contract that is in the accumulation stage and has been in force for less than five years may only surrender the policy where he requires immediate cash in circumstances of financial hardship.

What you leave to a named beneficiary goes to them directly

Money payable to the person you named passes outside your estate, and out of reach of anyone you owed.

The Act says
Where the policyholder is the life insured and where the proceeds of an insurance policy are payable to a beneficiary, such proceeds shall not form part of the policyholder's estate and shall not … be subject to the claim of any of the policyholder's creditors.
Worth checking: that the person named is still the person you intend. It is one of the first things the review confirms.

Your money

What the Act says about premiums you have paid, the receipts you hold, and where you stand.

A premium paid to your registered representative counts as paid to Guardian Life

Every premium you have handed to a registered representative, and every receipt you were given, stands. The insurer cannot treat your policy as unpaid because of it.

The Act says
Premiums or other payments due that are received by an agency, brokerage or a sales representative on behalf of an insurer shall be deemed to be received by the insurer notwithstanding any conditions or stipulations to the contrary.
And
If a premium is paid to an agency, brokerage or sales representative then the insurer shall not avoid liability under the contract of insurance for non-payment of premiums.
What to do: keep every receipt you have. If you would like to know exactly what Guardian Life has recorded as paid on your policy, ask the branch for your paid-to date — it is on your file and takes one call. And from here, if you have been paying a representative in person, the branch will show you the ways to pay Guardian Life directly, with Guardian Life's receipt every time.

A policy collected in person cannot lapse because nobody came to collect

If yours is a policy whose premium was collected from you in person — an industrial policy, the kind with a receipt book — it cannot be forfeited for premiums that went unpaid because no one came to collect them. The gap is the insurer's to close, not yours to explain. The branch will tell you whether your policy is of this kind, and how to pay Guardian Life directly from here.

The Act says
An industrial policy shall not be forfeited by reason only of the non-payment of any premium where the non-payment is as a result of non-collection by the insurer.

Money paid through a brokerage goes into a trust account, and must reach the insurer within ten business days

If you ever pay a premium through a brokerage, the Act requires it to hold your money in a separate consumer trust account, and on life business it may not take commission out of that account. Any agency, brokerage or sales representative that takes money for a life insurer must pass it on within ten business days. You are entitled to a receipt in the insurer's name every time.

The Act says
A brokerage shall establish and maintain a consumer trust account for the receipt and payment of consumer funds in respect of each type of insurance for which it is registered. … A brokerage transacting long-term insurance business shall not be permitted to deduct from a consumer trust account any commission and other deductions.
And
… shall pay over such monies to the insurer within ten business days from the date on which it received such monies.

The money behind your policy is held in a separate account for Trinidad and Tobago policyholders

A claim is paid by Guardian Life of the Caribbean from assets the Act requires it to keep in its Trinidad and Tobago policy account for its policyholders here — supervised by the Central Bank, which can order it topped up. A claim has never been paid by an agent, a broker or a branch.

The Act says
An insurer shall maintain adequate assets in its Trinidad and Tobago policy account to support its liabilities to its Trinidad and Tobago policyholders. … the Inspector may direct the insurer to maintain and hold such additional assets in its Trinidad and Tobago policy account as may be specified for the purpose of safeguarding the interests of Trinidad and Tobago policyholders.

Nothing an insurer or its people get wrong can invalidate your policy

Your policy is a contract in its own right. A breach of the Act by the company does not touch it, and if an insurer were ever wound up, what is owed on policies is paid ahead of the company's ordinary creditors.

The Act says
Failure on the part of an insurer to comply with any provision of this Act shall not in any way invalidate a policy issued by the insurer.

Who you can turn to

Two places, and neither is a menu.

The Central Bank of Trinidad and Tobago

The regulator for every insurer, agent and broker in the country. It keeps the public register, confirms whether a person is registered and for whom, and has the power to revoke a registration where the interests of policyholders are threatened. An approach you are not comfortable with can be reported there.

The Act says
The Central Bank shall make available to any person on request … a list of the insurers, agents, agencies, brokers, brokerages, sales representatives and adjusters registered either at the date of the request or at such earlier date.

The branch that looks after your policy

A person answers. Your birthday note, your premium reminder before a due date, and the review every six months carry on exactly as they have — every one of them dated and on your file. If you want anything on this page confirmed against your own policy, that is one call.
