Reading the branch

One screen you actually need, ten if you want them, five e-mails, and three numbers that are not what they look like. Fifteen minutes to read once, and you will never have to guess what a figure means again.

What this is

One place, built from the branch's own book, rebuilt every night.

The branch keeps its records in a Google workbook — premiums, pending business, requirements, the in-force book. All of it is there and none of it was readable. Four questions got asked every week and none could be answered from the sheet directly:

  • Which premiums are actually going to lapse, and whose are they?
  • What is stuck in pending, and what is stopping it?
  • Which policies mature soon — pensions and life, separately?
  • Which cover runs out before anybody notices?

Branch Intelligence answers those, plus a fifth nobody was asking: who should you call, and what should you say?

The one thing to know about how it works Every night at 2 a.m. it reads the workbook and works everything out. During the day it is reading last night's answers, not the sheet. A change somebody makes in the workbook this afternoon shows up tomorrow morning, not immediately. That is what makes it fast enough to be worth opening.

Three numbers that are not what they look like

Read this section even if you read nothing else.

1. "Premium" is one instalment, not a year

The premium column in the dues extract is one payment — one month for a monthly policy, one year for an annual one. The column that would tell you which is completely empty, in all 20,392 rows.

So the book cannot be annualised, and this app never pretends it can. Where you see instalments at risk, that is exactly what it means: add up one payment from each policy. It is not annual premium and it is not what the branch would lose.

Never do this Do not take "TT$6.9m at risk" into a meeting as an annual figure. On monthly business it understates by roughly twelve times, and mixed with annual policies it means nothing at all. Say "2,536 policies, past the grace period" — that number is exact.

2. Status 2 says "Premium Paying" and means overdue

The workbook has a status code and a status description, and on the overdue policies they disagree. Status 2 — the collectable book, the whole reason the chase list exists — carries the description "Premium Paying".

CodeWhat it really meansWhat you do
0No premium problem — read the description for what the policy actually isNothing. Not a dues case.
1Lapsed. Already gone.Reinstatement, not collection
2Overdue, still premium-payingThis is the chase list
3Pending, underwriting incompletePending business

If you ever filter the raw sheet yourself, filter on the code. Filtering on "Premium Paying" misses every case that matters.

3. A cross-sell "gap" is a gap in our book

When the app says a client has no retirement plan, it means no retirement plan with this branch. They may well have one with another company, another branch, or through their employer. The app cannot see those and does not claim to.

This changes how you open Never open with "you have no retirement plan". Open with "do you have anything set up for retirement anywhere?" The first is wrong often enough to cost you the relationship. The second is right every time and gets you the same conversation.

Everyone · when the phone rings

Find a client

Every other screen is a list. This one runs the other way round: somebody gives you a name and you get their whole story on one page — every policy, what each is worth, what is pending, what requirement is outstanding, and what the last person to speak to them wrote down.

Search on whatever they give you: a surname, the policy number off their letter, their client number, the phone they are calling from, or an e-mail. Several matches give you a list to pick from.

Do: use this before you call anybody back. It is the only screen that reads the workbook live rather than last night's rebuild, so a premium that came in this morning already shows.
Everyone · the good news

Settled

The one screen here about something going right. Every other screen is arrears, lapses and cases stuck somewhere — this is what actually made it onto the books, month by month, with who wrote it.

Know this: an application is the sum of the COUNT column, not a count of rows. That column is a flag — 1 on the base policy, 0 on each rider attached to it, −1 on a reversal — so a month showing 126 rows is 70 applications. If your own figure disagrees with the board, this is usually why.
Everyone · why the desk is busy

First time

How often a case is accepted exactly as submitted, with no referral and nothing sent back. Across the branch it is about a third. Everything else comes back — and that coming back is what fills the pending list and the requirements queue.

Do: read your own rate against the branch's, then look at what the referrals asked for. A point of straight-through is work that never arrives, rather than work done faster.
And know the limits: rates are this year only, agents under ten cases are not shown, and the median case size sits beside every rate — because bigger cases do get referred more, and the number is a question to ask, not a verdict.
Everyone

Premium dues

Policies more than 30 days overdue and still premium-paying. Under 30 days is inside the grace period and is deliberately left alone — chasing somebody who is not late costs you goodwill for nothing.

Three cuts underneath: how the arrears are aged, which billing method produced them, and which agents are carrying them.

Do: press Group into clients before you start calling. The list shows 2,536 policies but they belong to 1,838 people — one company scheme carries 34 policies behind a single phone number. Grouped, that is one row and one call.
Then: look at billing method before you look at the names. Pre Authorized Cheque fails at 42%, Bankers Order at 18%. Moving a client from a failing method to a working one stops them coming back every quarter — that is a fix, where a phone call is only a patch.

Everyone

Requirements

Outstanding requirements, oldest first, with the codes spelled out — FUTPY is first premium, DECLF is declaration of health, PRADD is proof of address. Those three are most of the queue, and all three are things an agent can collect without head office.

Do: notice that the median requirement has been open over 500 days. Most of those are cases that ended and were never closed off. Clearing them is what makes the live list believable.
Everyone

Maturities

Pensions and life kept apart, because they are two different conversations. A pension maturity is a decision about a fund. A life maturity is an endowment paying out.

Do: start pension maturities a year out. The decision — annuity, lump sum, or roll into a new plan — gets made months before the maturity letter reaches the client. Whoever is in the room when it is made keeps the money.
Everyone

Expiring cover

Term cover reaching its end date, and — the part nobody watches — the conversion right that ends with it.

While a convertible term policy runs, the client can move to permanent cover with no evidence of health. The day the term ends, that right is gone and a medical decides instead. By then they are older, and possibly uninsurable.

Do: treat the conversion list as the most valuable thing in this app. Nobody is using it, and it expires quietly.
Everyone

Cross-sell

Every client measured against what they already hold, one row each, under their strongest reason, scored out of 100. The score is about whether the call will happen — reachable, paid up, right age, proven capacity — not just how big the gap is.

ScoreMeans
80–100Call this week
60–79Worth a call
Under 60Keep on file

Two kinds of client never appear: anybody with no phone and no e-mail, and anybody more than 30 days behind on what they already hold. The second is a collections call. Pitching somebody in arrears is how a branch loses both sales.

Do: use the question in the last column as your opening. It asks instead of telling, for the reason in section 3 above.
Everyone · the urgent one

What moved

Each night the book is fingerprinted and compared with the night before. A policy that slid from overdue to lapsed looks identical to one that was always lapsed — this is the only place the difference shows.

Do: work Lapsed last night the same day. Reinstatement is easiest in the first weeks and hardest after a year. These are as easy as they will ever be, today. These lists group by client too — three policies lapsing on one person is one call, not three.
Managers, mainly

Data health

Not an appendix. Every figure in this app rests on an extract with known faults, and anybody acting on a number is entitled to know how solid it is.

Do: check it before you take a figure into a meeting. Each row says what is wrong, how many rows it affects, and where it gets fixed — which is almost always upstream, in how the export is produced.
And look at the top: How current each source is shows the newest dated record in every tab. The extracts do not refresh together — one of them has been known to sit two months behind while the others were current. A figure built on a stale tab describes that week, not this one.

Logging what happened

The one habit that makes this worth having.

Every row on every screen has a Log button. Use it after a call — the outcome, what was said, the next step, and a date to come back on.

That does three things. The next person who opens that policy sees it, so nobody rings a client twice. Your follow-up date comes back to you in your morning e-mail on the day. And the branch has a record of the work, which is otherwise invisible.

Where it goes Into the Intel Actions tab, under your name. That tab is never overwritten by the nightly rebuild — the watchlist tabs are rebuilt from scratch every night, so anything typed into those is lost. Notes belong in the app.

Working from the Sheet instead

If you would rather sort and filter in Google Sheets.

The nightly rebuild also writes six ordinary tabs into the workbook, carrying exactly the figures the app shows: Watchlist — Dues, — Pending, — Requirements, — Maturities, — Expiry and — Cross-sell.

They are rebuilt every night Anything you type into a watchlist tab is gone by morning. Sort them, filter them, copy from them — but record decisions with the Log button, or in Intel Actions.

When something looks wrong

What you seeWhat it usually is
"That login and code do not match"The code, or your row is marked inactive. The message is the same whether the login is unknown or the code is wrong — telling those apart would tell an outsider which logins exist. Ask the branch.
You are signed in but every screen is emptyYour name on the access list does not match the way it is spelled in the data. Ask the branch to check it against the dues tab. If you are staff rather than an agent, your Role on the access list needs to say so — staff have no book of their own and see the branch's instead.
Your dues show but your maturities and cross-sell are emptyYour policies are probably serviced under your company name. The app links the two by your agent code and rebuilds that link nightly — if it is still empty, your code is missing from the access list.
"No intelligence has been built yet"The nightly rebuild has not run. A manager can run it on demand from Branch Intelligence → Rebuild now in the workbook.
A figure looks too high or too lowCheck Data health first. Roughly one row in eight of the dues extract has something wrong with it, and the screen says exactly what.
A figure has not moved in weeksLook at How current each source is on Data health. If that tab's newest record is weeks old, the extract behind it has probably stopped refreshing — the app is reporting faithfully on old data.
A policy number shows as "number lost"Excel wrote it in scientific notation on export and the digits are gone. 107 rows are affected. It is fixed by formatting that column as plain text before the export is pasted.
A client is on the chase list with no contact at all351 of them are. Nothing can be collected from someone the branch cannot reach — finding a number for them is worth more than another call to somebody you can already reach.
