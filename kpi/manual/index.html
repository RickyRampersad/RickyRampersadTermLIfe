Why we changed

Not a new form. A different question.

The old tracker asked four times a day: what did you do? It was answered from memory at four o'clock, the Salesforce numbers were typed by hand, and it counted whether a box had been filled rather than whether any work had moved. We read our own book in Salesforce on 5 September and three things were plain.

Most of what is open is already in hand. Across the branch, only about one open task in three has gone a week without being touched. The rest is being worked. "Thirty-seven open" frightens people and tells them nothing about their morning. "Nine need you" is true, and can be acted on.
The clock is not the constraint. One desk clears seventeen tasks a day; another clears three, because its work is escalations and licensing rather than renewals. The same four two-hour blocks for both is a schedule, not a plan.
Retyping is the waste. Salesforce already holds what was done, on which task, for which client. Asking a person to write it out again at four o'clock produced "3 tasks" and a name — and cost the branch the record it thought it was keeping.

So the model turned around. The day is planned before it starts, against the book as it actually is. Each block shows the work itself. At the close, one question. And the record of what was done lives where it was done — in Salesforce — with the tracker asking only whether the objective landed.

The principle under all of it Focus on what matters, not on activity for its own sake. The tracker's job is to put in front of you the smallest true list of what needs you today, and to get out of the way.

The shape of a day

The same rhythm for everyone. Only the work inside it differs.

When you arrive
Sign in. That is your attendance. The first sign-in of the day is the time you started; there is no separate register and nothing else to fill. Eight o'clock desks and nine o'clock desks are each read against their own hours. If you are not in, sign in anyway and press Not in today? — sick, on leave, or what it is. Your People Leader can record it for you if you phoned in.
Then
Set the day before it starts. Your first sign-in opens on the plan, with your open book already read from Salesforce. Tick what each block is for, see how much of it actually needs you and how long that is at your own pace, and press Start the day.
Each block
Work the list, then close it. The block shows its tasks. As each one moves, the record goes on the task in Salesforce. When the block ends, answer Was the objective met? — Met, Partly, or Not yet — and submit. A receipt lands in your inbox.
12:00
If a morning block has not been submitted, one reminder. If both are in, nothing — you will not be written to for work you have already done.
15:00
The Branch Manager's 3pm checkpoint goes out: who has reported, who is behind, the branch's task position, live from Salesforce. Anyone with three blocks still outstanding gets one chase.
Before you leave
Save the day. Value added, an idea if you had one, anything to flag. Three fields; most days two are "none".
Friday 17:00
The weekly summary: the week person by person, where the time went, value added, ideas, training, blockers, and movement on the week before.
How long it takes Two minutes to plan the morning. Under a minute to close a block. The rest of the day is the work, not the reporting of it.

The morning plan

Every line on the screen, and what it is telling you.

Your first sign-in of the day opens here. It is the same four blocks you have always had, with their times — what has changed is that each one now shows you the size and the shape of the work before you commit two hours to it.

The chip: "4 need you of 17, 1 late"

Need you is the number to plan around. It counts open tasks of that type that nobody has touched in seven days. An open task modified yesterday is in somebody's hands — the same task untouched for three weeks is not, whatever its due date says. When nothing has gone quiet, the chip says "17 open, all in hand", and that is a good morning.

Late is past its due date. In the branch is the same type across everyone, so you can tell whether your three are the whole picture or your corner of it.

The estimate: "About 2 hours at your rate"

Built from what you actually clear in a day, from your own last sixty days — not a branch average, and not a guess about minutes. It shows its working beside it so it can be argued with. Where the history is too thin to mean anything, no estimate is offered at all.

If the work will not fill focused time, it says so: "About half an hour at your rate — not a two-hour block on its own." That is the cue to pair it with something, not to stretch it.

What is in it

The tasks themselves, untouched-first, in the order they fall due, with the agent each is for. Where one agent holds three or more, that is named on its own line: six chases to one agent is one conversation, not six.

Late, and no reason on it yet

Below the blocks, only the exceptions: overdue tasks carrying no reason, grouped by agent. Nothing is asked about work that is running fine.

How long do you think this needs?

Your own estimate, made with the counts in front of you. It is kept against the outcome you give at the close, and over the coming weeks that pairing — what you planned against what landed — becomes the branch's most honest measure of how long our work really takes.

Signed in again later A second sign-in opens on the day, with the plan one tap away at the top: Plan the day. Only the first sign-in of the day plans it.

Working a block

The list, the reason, the close.

1

The work is on the task, not in the box. As each task moves, put a line on it in Salesforce — in the description, or as a Chatter note. Either counts. Neither is optional. A task moved forward with nothing written on it is not a record that anything was done.

2

An overdue task with no reason gets a box. The tracker lists them — Overdue with no reason given — with one line to fill: what it is waiting on, and who owns the next step. What you write goes straight into the task's Task Update Reason in Salesforce. A rolled date is not an actioned task.

3

Three lines, which are not the same line. Actioned — which matter, and what moved. Resolved / closed — what came of it, or "none today", which is an honest answer. Still open & owned by you — what you are carrying forward, and if it is waiting on somebody, who, and the date you last chased.

4

Was the objective met? Met, Partly, or Not yet. If not, one line on what stopped it. That is the whole close. Then Submit block.

The standard for anything you write How many, or which one. A count, or a case. "Actioned remaining tasks" is neither. "5 pending cases chased, 3 replied with documents, 1 lapsing Friday" is both. If a block reads thin the tracker asks once, and then sends whatever you write — it is a prompt, not a gate.

If the block included training, four more fields open: who, what was covered, objectives set against met, and the check or test. Those go to the training register, which is the record the branch reports from.

What is required of you

By role. Your KPI list is your job document, in the order your day runs.

The types you tick are not a menu somebody invented for the tracker. Ten of them are the Salesforce task types the branch already files every task under, so they carry live numbers. The rest are the responsibilities in your job document that Salesforce does not hold — those are written in words, and no number is expected against them.

EveryoneEach morningEach blockEach day
Whatever your role Sign in when you arrive — that is your attendance. Tick what each block is for. Read "need you" before "open". Give each block an honest estimate. Start the day. Work the list. A line on every task you touch, in Salesforce. A reason on every overdue. Answer "did it land" and submit before the next block starts. Save the day before you leave. Read your receipts — they are your copy of your own record.

Sales Support Assistant

You own: the new application process · client portfolios and macros · document management (scan, classify, verify) · reporting · administrative support · mail and contracts · quotations and proposals · the orphan adoption listing · OFT collation · surveys and Query Pal · task management.

Your book is mostly case work: pendings, renewals and billing, servicing lines. It is the fastest-moving book in the branch and, on the day we read it, the cleanest — the tracker should say "all in hand" often. When it says "need you", that is the exception to go to first.

What you see: your own days, your own book, your own trend. Your team's position is on the office wall and in the Branch Manager's checkpoint.

Personal Assistant to the Branch Manager

You own: mail and contracts · document delivery and collection · the diary, appointments and travel · Branch Manager support · scripts and transmittals · banking, errands and office runs · supplies and facilities · meeting preparation and branch events.

Your book is movement: most of it never becomes a Salesforce task, so most of your blocks are written in words — where a thing went, and who has it now. The Salesforce-backed types on your list are the scripts and transmittals and the RR Operations items; those carry numbers.

What you see: your own days and book.

Branch Manager's Assistant

You own: new application oversight · surveys and Query Pal · pending, re-date and persistency · new agent activity (recruitment, OFT, licensing) · reporting (production, RDAR) · budget and expense · people management and staff leadership · orphan policy management · issues resolution and future payments · administrative support · escalations · task distribution.

Your book is the heaviest per item in the branch. Licensing, staffing and HR, escalations, claims. Your rate will read low beside a support desk's and that is the shape of the work, not a judgement — the estimate is built against your pace, not theirs. When "need you" is high on Licensing / Staffing / SA / HR, that is where the branch's risk sits.

Task distribution is yours. Work arriving untyped, or landing on the wrong desk, is yours to file and to move. The wall's "untyped inbound" card is a measure of this.

What you see: your own days and book. The support team's position is on the wall and in the Branch Manager's checkpoint; the daily DILO you send them is now the block schedule the tracker shows.

Unit Manager

You own: unit recruitment · joint field work · unit production and activity · agent licensing and development · unit persistency · your personal production and own book · the unit meeting and coaching · client escalations.

Most of your day is not a Salesforce task, and it should not be forced into one. Field work, one-on-ones and coaching are written in words, and the standard is the same: which agent, what was covered, what was agreed. The Salesforce-backed types that do apply — opportunity, pendings, licensing — carry numbers.

Your unit's persistency is a KPI on your list. Log it against the block it was worked in, with the case names.

What you see: your own days and book. Your unit's agents are not tracker users; your view of them is Salesforce and the wall.

Assistant Branch Manager

You own: recruitment intake and selection · agent school and onboarding · unit manager supervision · joint field work · production and pipeline · persistency and conservation · agent licensing and compliance · personal production · client escalations · reporting (production, RDAR) · deputising for the Branch Manager.

You carry the programmes. Intake, agent school, licensing — work with a calendar rather than a task list. Block it, name the cohort and the stage, and say what moved.

When you deputise, you deputise for the rhythm too: the 3pm checkpoint and the wall are read by whoever is running the branch that day.

What you see: your own days and book. The branch view — every person's days, the checkpoint, the weekly, the wall — is the Branch Manager's, and yours when the Branch Manager's address is set to you for the day.

Branch Manager

You own: recruitment and selection · agent development and field training · production and pipeline · activity management (one-on-ones, joint field work) · persistency and conservation · compliance, licensing and market conduct · budget and expense · the branch team · your personal production and own book · client escalations · reporting (production, RDAR, branch meeting) · business planning.

Your book is the branch's largest and its least typed, and the day we read it, most of its overdue sat there. Two things follow. Give every task on your desk a type — an untyped task is invisible to every count on every screen. And get the case work off your desk: a billing task belongs on a support desk, and the tracker will show you whose.

Your servicing runs are counted, and counted separately. A run that reaches hundreds of clients at once is real work and a real contact — it is reported on its own line, never netted against case work and never subtracted from it.

What you see: everything. Every person's days and book, the 3pm checkpoint, the weekly summary, trends per person, and the wall. That reach is yours alone, so that what is on the wall was put there by one accountable pair of eyes.

Where the job document goes next Your KPI list already is your job document. The quarterly appraisal will read from this same record — closed, in hand, late, and your own planned-against-met — so that when the quarter is reviewed, it is measured against the document you were handed and the days you actually logged, not against recollection.

Your performance

The evaluation form, with the daily record beside it.

Guardian's Performance Evaluation form has three parts: weighted performance goals rated one to five, eight behavioural competencies rated one to five, and development on the 70-20-10 model. My performance on the tracker is that form, and under every goal it puts what the daily record says for the period: blocks worked and how many landed, tasks of that kind closed in Salesforce, and what is open, untouched or late now. That is what the rating is made against. It is never the rating.

The goals are your KPI types with weights

For a Sales Support Assistant the ten goals on the form are the same work the tracker already counts — new applications, premium dues, contracts and scripts, mail by three, reporting — each with a weight that adds to a hundred and a target. Tick the right type on your blocks and the evidence gathers itself.

Two halves, and only your own

You rate yourself and write your note. Your People Leader — whoever you report to, or the Branch Manager — rates you and writes theirs. Neither of you can touch the other's column. Both are on one page, because a review is a conversation.

The number, exactly as the form makes it

A goal scores its weight times the rating over four, so "Meets Expectations" is the whole weight. Competencies are the average rating over four. The overall rating is eighty percent goals and twenty percent competencies, and Guardian's minimum standard is seventy-two. The People Leader's rating is the rating of record; the screen shows the working beside it.

Development on 70-20-10

Seventy percent of learning is on the job, twenty from other people, ten formal. Your People Leader writes each action in three parts — what you will do, why, and what success looks like — and you mark it done. Your training plan sits on the same screen, with its evidence and sign-off.

When

Mid probation, end of probation, then quarterly, with mid-year and end-of-year as the form has them. Your People Leader opens the review for the period; you write your half first, they write theirs, and both sign. A signed review does not change afterwards.

Where it lives The form's goals and competencies, and every rating, are in the branch workbook — never on the public site. The tracker only reads and writes them for the person and their People Leader.

Your quarter, every day

The appraisal form asks two questions: what did you deliver against each weighted goal, and how did you show each competency. The tracker answers both as you go, so the quarterly is a read-off and not a memory test.

Under your blocks on the day screen sits Your quarter: every goal on your form with what the record says beneath it — blocks worked and whether they landed, what Salesforce closed, what is open and untouched — and each competency with how much already sits behind it. The plan screen says which goals are behind before you choose the day's blocks, so the day is planned against them rather than in spite of them.

What the record can see, it counts. Reliability reads your attendance and your blocks. Responsiveness reads what is untouched, late, or late with no reason. Quality reads whether blocks landed and the billing flags. Customer service reads your value-added lines and servicing closed. Growth reads training received and development actions done. Innovation reads your innovation lines. None of it is a rating; it is what you and your People Leader rate against.

What the record cannot see, you note. A client calmed down, a colleague helped at four o'clock, a form redesigned so it stops coming back: press Note a moment, pick the competency it showed, write a line. Your People Leader can note one for you. Moments sit under the competency, dated, in every review that covers that day.

The habit A moment takes twenty seconds on the day and is unrecoverable three months later. Note it when it happens.

The rules

Short. Agreed. Not optional.

  • Sign in when you arrive. That is the register. Not in? Sign in and say why.
  • A line on every task you touch. Description or Chatter. A task moved with nothing written on it did not happen.
  • A reason on every overdue. What it is waiting on and who owns the next step. In the box the tracker gives you, which writes it to Salesforce.
  • A block is submitted when it ends, not at four o'clock from memory.
  • How many, or which one. A count or a case in every line you write.
  • Waiting on somebody is not a blocker until it has a name and a date last chased.
  • An untyped task is an invisible task. File it, or it is in nobody's count.
  • Two kinds of work are never added together or subtracted from each other. Case work, one at a time. Servicing runs, hundreds at once. Both are the branch's work; they are not the same measurement.
  • No estimate is better than a wrong one. Where the tracker has too little history it says nothing, and so should you.

If something goes wrong

In this order.

"Salesforce could not be reached, so type them for now."

The tracker keeps working without Salesforce: the counts go dark and the three position numbers become boxes to type. Everything else — the blocks, the close, the receipts — carries on. Tell the Branch Manager; it is usually the connection, not you.

The chip says "17 open" but no tasks are listed under the block

The page is newer than the script behind it. The Branch Manager redeploys the script; nothing on your side.

You typed a block and the page closed

Sign in again. What you typed is still there — it is saved on the phone as you type.

"Another submission is going in just now"

Somebody else pressed Submit in the same second. It retries by itself; you will see it counting. Do nothing.

The block "reads thin"

It is asking for a count or a case. Add one, or press Send it anyway — it is a prompt, not a gate.

"Too many attempts"

Five wrong passwords. Wait fifteen minutes, or ask the Branch Manager.
