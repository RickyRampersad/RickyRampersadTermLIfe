If a client says…

The things a client may repeat to you in the weeks ahead, what is simply true in each case, and the one warm sentence that answers it. Nothing here names or describes anyone. You never have to argue; you only ever have to be accurate, and then go back to their policy.

The shape of every answer

1 · Do not react. 2 · Say what is true, in one sentence, with no adjectives. 3 · Offer to show them — their file, the page, the comparison in writing. 4 · Back to their policy and the two doors. The client should come off the call feeling looked after, not recruited.

About the relationship

You may hear“I sold you this policy. I know your family. Come with me.”
What is simply trueThe policy stays with Guardian Life whoever sold it. It cannot go with anyone — a life policy is not transferable. Moving means ending it and buying a new one.
What you say“That relationship is yours to keep. Your policy isn't going anywhere either — it's exactly where it was, and so are we.”
You may hear“The branch never knew you. I was the only one who ever called.”
What is simply trueEvery contact from the branch is logged and dated on the client's file — the birthday note, the premium reminders before a due date, the service reviews, the maturity and conversion letters.
What you say“Let me go through what's on your file with you — every note has a date.” Then do it.
You may hear“Nobody there will look after you now.”
What is simply trueThe client is matched to a named, registered agent within two working days of asking, and the branch desk answers in between.
What you say“You'll have a name, not a department — mine, if you'd like it.”

About a broker

You may hear“As a broker I act for you now, not for the insurer. I can shop the whole market.”
What is simply trueA broker arranges new insurance. A broker cannot transfer an existing policy any more than an agent can. Shopping the market means a new policy, priced at today's age and underwritten on today's health.
What you say“A new policy is a fair thing to consider — with the advantages and the disadvantages set out in writing, as the Act requires, next to what you already have.”
You may hear“I can get you a better deal elsewhere.”
What is simply trueA premium fixed years ago at a younger age cannot be bought again. A “better deal” has to be compared against that price, the paid-up value, any waiver of premium, and the three-year clock a new policy restarts.
What you say“Ask for the comparison in writing. The Act says it has to be complete — including what you'd give up.”
You may hear“I'll still look after your Guardian policy for you.”
What is simply trueRegistration is tied to the company a person represents; when that contract ends the Central Bank revokes it. Anything on a Guardian Life policy — a beneficiary change, a loan, a claim — is done through Guardian Life.
What you say“Anything on this policy comes through Guardian Life, and I can do it with you — today if you like.”

About a better product

You may hear“Your policy is old. The new plans are much better.”
What is simply trueAn older policy carries the price of the age it started at, protection against an honest error in the application after three years, and the riders it was issued with. A new one starts all of that from zero.
What you say“Let's look at what yours has actually built. It's usually more than people think.”
You may hear“More cover for less money.”
What is simply trueFor the same person, older, cover costs more, not less. Less premium usually means less cover, a shorter term, or fewer riders — the comparison shows which.
What you say“Put the two side by side — sum assured, term, riders, premium — and I'll go through the comparison with you line by line.”
You may hear“Cash it in and start fresh.”
What is simply trueAfter three years of premiums the client is entitled to a paid-up policy instead of a surrender. Surrendering gives up the cover and any waiver of premium with it.
What you say“Before anyone cashes anything, ask what the paid-up value is. It's the option people are never told about.”

About the branch

You may hear“The branch is in chaos. Look how many of us left.”
What is simply trueThe service to the client — reminders, birthday notes, reviews, claims — has continued without a break, and the record is dated. Nothing about the client's policy has changed.
What you say“What matters is what happens to your policy, and nothing has changed. Here's your file.”
You may hear“The manager pushed us out.” — or anything else about how anyone left.
What is simply trueNothing about how anyone left changes a policy, a premium, a beneficiary or a claim. It is not yours to discuss and it is not the client's problem.
What you say“I wasn't part of that and I won't speak about it. I can speak about your policy.” And then do.
You may hear“Guardian is losing its agents — get out while you can.”
What is simply trueAn insurer's obligations under a policy do not depend on which agents it employs. Under the Act a breach by the insurer never invalidates a policy, and policyholders rank ahead of ordinary creditors.
What you say“Your policy is a contract with Guardian Life. It doesn't depend on who works where.”

About fear

You may hear“Your claim won't get paid. Nobody there knows your file.”
What is simply trueA claim is paid from the policy and the file, and both are with Guardian Life. The client's history, contact record and beneficiary details are on record.
What you say“Your file is here. Would you like me to show you what's on it — and confirm your beneficiary while we're at it?”
You may hear“You're an orphan policy now. Nobody's responsible for you.”
What is simply trueThe branch has matched the client to a named agent — you — and stands behind the assignment. The promise is a name within two working days, and it has been kept.
What you say“You've got a name now — mine — and the branch behind it.”
You may hear“If you don't move now you'll lose out.”
What is simply trueThere is no deadline on keeping a policy. The only clocks that reset are the ones a new policy starts.
What you say“There's no deadline on keeping what you have. Take your time — we'll ask again rather than assume.”

About money that changed hands

You may hear“I paid my premiums to my agent in cash. Are they even on the policy?”
What is simply trueUnder the Act a premium paid to a registered representative is deemed received by the insurer, and the receipt is the insurer's receipt. The branch checks the paid-to date against the file and tells the client what it shows.
What you say“Keep every receipt. Let me check your paid-to date now, and I'll show you the ways to pay Guardian Life directly from here on, with Guardian Life's receipt every time.”
Never, in any of these
  • Name, describe, or explain anyone who has left. “Has moved on from Guardian Life” is the whole reference.
  • Say “that's not true”, “they're lying”, or anything about anyone's honesty. Show the file instead.
  • Quote fines or prison terms. The client has the protections page; it carries the Act's words. You carry the warmth.
  • Warn the client that someone will approach them. Ask whether someone has; nothing more.
  • Tell a client they are in arrears, or that money paid to a representative is missing, until the branch has reconciled the file.
After every one of these conversations Log approached on the client's row, with the date and the one line that was said to them. Not who said it. The pattern across the book is what the branch needs; the name is not.
