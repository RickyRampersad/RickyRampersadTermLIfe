If a client says…

The things a client may repeat to you in the weeks ahead, what is simply true in each case, and the one warm sentence that answers it. Nothing here names or describes anyone. You never have to argue; you only ever have to be accurate, and then go back to their policy.

The shape of every answer

1 · Do not react. 2 · Concede whatever is true in what they were told. 3 · Say what is simply true, in one sentence, with no adjectives. 4 · Offer to show them — their file, the protections page, the comparison in writing. 5 · Back to their policy and the two doors. The client should come off the call feeling looked after, not recruited.

About the relationship

You may hear“I sold you this policy. I know your family. Come with me — nothing changes for you except who picks up the phone.”
What is simply trueNothing changes only if the client stays. The policy stays with Guardian Life whoever sold it — a life policy is not transferable. A “move” is a surrender, a new application at today's age and health, a fresh three-year period under the Act, and the loss of the issue-age price, the protection already earned and any waiver of premium.
What you say“That relationship is yours to keep. Nothing needs to change for you at all — your policy stays exactly where it is. If you ever want to look at something new, ask for the two set out side by side in writing, and I'll go through it with you.”
You may hear“The branch never knew you. I was the only one who ever called.”
What is simply trueEvery contact from the branch is logged and dated on the client's file — the birthday note, the premium reminders before a due date, the service reviews, the maturity and conversion letters. On some files that record is full; on some it is thin. Read it before you answer.
What you say“Let me go through what's on your file with you — every note has a date.” Then do it. And if the file is thin: “You're right that you haven't heard from us enough. That changes today — here is my number, and here is when I'll call.” Nothing disarms a complaint like agreeing with the true part of it.
You may hear“Nobody there will look after you now. Your file goes in a drawer with nobody's name on it.”
What is simply trueThe policy is looked after by the branch under the manager's name from the day the representative left, and a named agent follows within two working days of the client asking — or on this call, if you are the one making it. The client may choose the agent by name. A missed premium triggers the Act's written notice and twenty business days; any letter the client needs — school, bank, embassy — is issued by Guardian Life through the branch, as before.
What you say“You have a name now — mine — and you didn't have to click anything to get it. If there's someone at the branch you'd rather, say the name and it's done. The reminder before a premium and any letter you ever need come from us, the same as they always have.”
You may hear“I got you through underwriting the first time, remember? I'll do it again — I know how to present you.”
What is simply trueUnderwriting is done on the age and health at the date of the new application. A condition diagnosed since the first policy can load the premium, add an exclusion or decline the case, and nobody can present a case out of that. The policy the client holds is already issued and can never be underwritten again.
What you say“The good news is you've already been through underwriting — this policy is issued, and nothing about your health today can touch it.”

About a broker

You may hear“As a broker I act for you now, not for the insurer. I can shop the whole market.”
What is simply trueDo not answer “that's not true” — the Act's own definition says a broker arranges insurance on behalf of policyholders. What is simply true: a broker arranges new insurance and cannot move an existing policy any more than an agent can; a broker is paid on the policy that is placed; a brokerage must disclose in writing any special arrangement it holds with an insurer; and the Act binds brokers and brokerages to the same advantages-and-disadvantages discussion as agents.
What you say“A broker is a fair thing to consider. Whoever advises you, me included, is paid on the policy that gets placed — so ask both of us the same two things, which companies and what we'd earn, and let's both put it in writing.”
You may hear“You were only ever shown one company's price.”
What is simply trueTrue, and the branch says so on the protections page. The premium the client pays was fixed at the age and health of the issue date and is a price nobody, Guardian Life included, can quote today; any new quote has to be set beside it, not against a same-company price.
What you say“That's right — we only place Guardian Life, and we say so. The price you're paying is one nobody, including us, can quote you today, so anything new has to be set beside it, line for line.”
You may hear“I'll still look after your Guardian policy for you.”
What is simply trueRegistration is tied to the company a person represents; when that contract ends the Central Bank revokes it. Anything on a Guardian Life policy — a beneficiary change, a loan, a claim — is done through Guardian Life. Promise only that: the branch must confirm with Guardian Life what it records as the servicing intermediary before anyone says “it's just a note on your file”.
What you say“Anything on this policy comes through Guardian Life, and I can do it with you — today if you like.”
You may hear“The policy is yours, not Guardian's. Bring it with you — I'll handle every piece of the paperwork.”
What is simply trueA life policy is a contract with the insurer that issued it and cannot be assigned to another company; “bringing it” is a surrender followed by a new application. Who looks after it is changed at Guardian Life without surrendering anything.
What you say“You never have to end a policy to change who looks after it. I'd be glad to be the name on it — or to help you choose someone else.”

About a better product

You may hear“Your policy is old. The new plans are much better.”
What is simply trueAn older policy carries the price of the age it started at, protection against an honest error in the application after three years, and the riders it was issued with. A new one starts all of that from zero.
What you say“Let's look at what yours has actually built. It's usually more than people think.”
You may hear“More cover for less money.”
What is simply trueIt might be, and an agent who says “impossible” loses the room. What is certain: a replacement is priced at the age and evidence of health at the date of application, every condition diagnosed since is disclosed and then rated, excluded or declined, and the three-year period restarts. The written comparison must hold sum assured, term and riders constant, with the same date of birth on both.
What you say“It might be — and the only way to know is to put them side by side on the same sum assured, same term and same riders. Ask for that in writing and I'll go through it with you line by line.”
You may hear“Cash it in and start fresh. It costs you nothing to switch.”
What is simply true“Costs you nothing” is the part that is not true. A surrender pays the cash value less any policy debt and any surrender penalties — the Act's own words — which in the early and middle years sits below the premiums paid. After three years of premiums the client is entitled to a paid-up policy instead. A new plan starts from zero after its own set-up years and restarts the three-year period. The figures are yours to fetch, not the client's to guess.
What you say“Before anyone decides, let me get you three numbers in writing: what you've paid in, what a surrender would pay today, and what the paid-up value is. The gap between them is the real cost of starting over.”
You may hear“Yours only pays when you die. The new ones come with living benefits.”
What is simply trueThe client may already hold some of it — the agent brief carries the riders on file; read the rider schedule before the call. Whether a waiver of premium or serious-illness cover can be added to the policy in force is a product question: check with Guardian Life before you say so.
What you say“Let's read your rider schedule together first — you may already have some of that. What you don't have, I'll check whether it can be added to this policy without giving anything up, and tell you straight.”
You may hear“You won't lose a day of cover — it's a simple switch.”
What is simply trueA new application is underwritten on today's health, can be rated, deferred or declined, and takes weeks to issue; a surrender signed today ends cover today. Same-day surrender-plus-application is the one sequence a client should never be asked to sign.
What you say“Whatever you decide, don't let this one end until any new one has been issued and is in your hands. It costs nothing to wait, and I'll keep this in force for you in the meantime.”

About the branch

You may hear“The branch is in chaos. Look how many of us left.”
What is simply trueThe client's policy is a contract with Guardian Life whose cover, premium, beneficiary and claims process do not change with the number of agents at any branch. A claim is paid by Guardian Life from the assets the Act requires it to hold in its Trinidad and Tobago policy account — never by an agent, a broker or a branch. Read the file before you say anything about service.
What you say“How many people left, and why, isn't something I'll speak to. Your family's claim has never been in any agent's hands — mine included; it's paid by Guardian Life, from a fund the law makes it keep. Let me go through your file with you, note by note.”
You may hear“The manager pushed us out.” — or anything else about how anyone left.
What is simply trueNothing about how anyone left changes a policy, a premium, a beneficiary or a claim. It is not yours to discuss. Do not claim distance you do not have — “I wasn't part of that” is a sentence anyone who was in the branch cannot say truthfully. The file itself — policy, contact record, beneficiary, premiums recorded — is held by Guardian Life, protected by the Act's confidentiality rule, and can be read back on request.
What you say“How anyone left isn't mine to discuss. Your file is — it's held by Guardian Life, and I can read it back to you now, beneficiary and all.”
You may hear“Guardian is losing its agents — get out while you can.”
What is simply trueAn insurer's obligations under a policy do not depend on which agents it employs. Under the Act a breach by the insurer never invalidates a policy, and policyholders rank ahead of ordinary creditors.
What you say“Your policy is a contract with Guardian Life. It doesn't depend on who works where.”
You may hear“The branch can only sell you Guardian, so of course they'll tell you to stay.”
What is simply trueConcede it. The branch represents Guardian Life only and the protections page says so. Being matched to a servicing agent costs the client nothing, changes no term, and earns the branch no new premium; keeping a policy is not a sale.
What you say“That's true, and I'll say it myself: I can only offer Guardian Life. Which is why I'm not asking you to buy anything — keeping what you have costs you nothing and changes nothing, and a comparison in writing is yours to ask for from anyone.”

About fear

You may hear“Your claim won't get paid. Nobody there knows your file.”
What is simply trueA claim is paid from the policy and the file, and both are with Guardian Life — the application, the medical evidence, the beneficiary, every premium recorded as paid. The client's contact history is on record.
What you say“Your file is here. Would you like me to show you what's on it — and confirm your beneficiary while we're at it?”
You may hear“You're an orphan policy now. Nobody's responsible for you.”
What is simply trueThe word is real trade usage and must not be argued with; it describes the absence of a servicing agent, not the client's standing. The gap closes on this call. Under the Act the policy stands on the contract with the insurer; cover, premium and beneficiaries are unchanged.
What you say“That's the word the trade uses when a representative leaves — it describes the gap, not you, and the gap closes now: you've got a name, mine, and you didn't have to click anything to get it.”
You may hear“If you don't move now you'll lose out.”
What is simply trueThere is no deadline on keeping a policy. The only clocks that reset are the ones a new policy starts.
What you say“There's no deadline on keeping what you have. Take your time — we'll ask again rather than assume.”

About money

You may hear“I paid my premiums to my agent in cash. Who's going to stand behind those receipts now? Move before somebody says you're in arrears.”
What is simply trueUnder the Act a premium paid to a registered representative is deemed received by the insurer, the receipt is the insurer's receipt, and the insurer cannot avoid liability for non-payment on that basis — so the receipts stand whether a receipt book was returned or not. Moving changes nothing about what Guardian Life has recorded as paid, and a new policy elsewhere inherits none of it. The answer to “arrears” is the paid-to date on the file, not a new application — and the branch reconciles the file before anyone tells a client they owe anything.
What you say“Every receipt you hold stands in law, and nothing about moving would change what's recorded as paid here. Let me check your paid-to date with you now, and from here I'll show you how to pay Guardian Life directly, with its own receipt every time.”
You may hear“First month free.” “I'll cover the first premium.” “There's a gift for signing.”
What is simply trueRebating is prohibited: no insurer's staff, agent, broker or sales representative may agree a premium other than the one in the policy, or give or offer any rebate, consideration or thing of value to a person insured or applying. The offer itself is unlawful — which is also why the branch offers nothing to stay.
What you say“The only premium that exists is the one written on the policy — for any company, including us. Ask for whatever's been offered to be shown on the policy document itself.”
You may hear“Move your savings plan across to me.”
What is simply trueAn annuity in its accumulation stage that has been in force for less than five years may only be surrendered where the owner needs immediate cash in circumstances of financial hardship. A “transfer” of a young annuity is proposing something the Act restricts. Check the product type on the sheet, never the product name.
What you say“If yours is a savings annuity under five years old, the Act only allows it to be cashed in for genuine hardship — so let's look at what it has built and what it's due to do, before anything else.”
Never, in any of these
  • Name, describe, or explain anyone who has left. “Has moved on from Guardian Life” is the whole reference.
  • Count. Not how many left, not when, not why. Any number — even a smaller one — concedes that the number is the question.
  • Say “that's not true”, “they're lying”, or anything about anyone's honesty. Show the file instead.
  • Claim distance you do not have. Refuse to discuss; do not deny.
  • Quote fines or prison terms. The client has the protections page; it carries the Act's words. You carry the warmth.
  • Warn the client that someone will approach them. Ask whether someone has; nothing more.
  • Quote a figure from memory, or say what another company would or would not offer. Numbers go in writing.
  • Tell a client they are in arrears, or that money paid to a representative is missing, until the branch has reconciled the file.
After every one of these conversations Log approached on the client's row, with the date and the one line that was said to them. Not who said it. The pattern across the book is what the branch needs; the name is not.

The Act's period for an honest error in the application is three years, not two; the sheet, the protections page and the film all say three. A suicide clause is a policy term, not an Act provision, and is never quoted as law.
