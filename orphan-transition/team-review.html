Why this, and why today

The clients are the point. Everything here is built so that not one of them is left wondering who looks after them.

What goes out

One of thirteen letters, chosen by what the client holds — a policy that has matured or is about to, a policy in force (in five versions by how long they have held it, or a check letter where cover ended and started again), a premium showing as due, a contract not yet delivered, an application in progress, or a policy that lapsed. Every one says, second line, that the representative has moved on and the policy has not; each opens with something the client is glad to hear. The one exception is letter T, for the book of an agent whose contract Guardian Life terminated in writing: its second line is the company's own notice, and nothing more.

What every letter carries

The facts for that client, read off the sheet: held since, paid to, days outstanding. Taps they answer with one thumb, always including the door into the questionnaire for anyone who wants an agent now and wants to say why first. The film, in one line. A plain note on what the Insurance Act already gives them. How the branch has looked after them. And the branch manager's name at the foot.

What comes back

Every click lands on the branch sheet with the segment attached. We match each client to one of you by town and by household, you get a brief, and the file is done when a review is signed on form 2000-03-147.

The one rule the whole set is built on

Nothing in any letter, the film, the page or the script is about anyone who has left. “Your representative has moved on from Guardian Life” is the entire reference. We do not explain, describe, or warn. We tell the client what is true about their own policy, what the law gives them, and who is here. A client who is treated that way does not need to be argued with.

Straight from the branch's own activity log, 1 January to today. Every entry is dated and sits on the client's file. If a client ever asks what the branch has done for them, this is the answer, and it is not an opinion.

All of it in the log, all of it dated. The letters lean on that record without boasting about it — they simply say the birthday note and the premium reminder carry on, because they do.

How to use this with a client

Never as a defence. If a client says nobody from the branch ever contacted them, the right answer is warm and specific: “Let me check your file — I can tell you exactly what went out and when.” Then check it. The dated record does the talking, and you did not have to. And where the record is thin — some files are — agree with the true part and make today the first entry: “You're right, you haven't heard from us enough. That changes today.”

What to check, in four questions

You know these clients better than a spreadsheet does. That is what we need from you.

1 · Would your own client be glad to get this?

Not tolerate it. Glad. If the opening line would not make one of your clients pick up the phone to thank you, say which line would.

2 · Is there one word that sounds defensive?

Or one word about anyone who left, beyond “has moved on”. If you find one, quote it. That is the word that undoes the letter.

3 · Is anything untrue for a client you know?

The letters make claims about the policy — a waiver, a paid-up value, a price fixed at the starting age. If you know a case where that is not how the product works, we need it before the send.

4 · Is the ask clear?

Three taps, one thumb. If you had to read the letter twice to find what it wants you to do, the client will not read it twice.

How we come across on the first contact

The client decides in the first thirty seconds whether they are being looked after or fought over. These are the rules for those thirty seconds.

1 · Open with their policy, not with the change

Do: “I'm calling about something on your file you should know about.” Then the thing they are glad to hear — the waiver, the price they are locked in at, the money waiting.
Don't: open with “your agent has left”. It is the second thing you say, in one sentence, and only once.

2 · One sentence about the change, and nothing you cannot say truthfully

Do: “Your representative has moved on from Guardian Life. Your policy is completely unaffected.” If asked what happened: “How anyone left isn't mine to discuss. Your policy is — here's your file.”
Don't: explain, agree, disagree, or claim distance you do not have. “I wasn't part of that” is a sentence anyone who was in the branch cannot say truthfully, and a client who later learns it has caught you in the one thing this whole approach depends on: accuracy.

3 · Read the file before you describe it

Do: “Let me go through what's on your file with you — every note has a date.” Then go through what is actually there. If it is thin: “You're right that you haven't heard from us enough. That changes today — here is my number and here is when I'll call.”
Don't: “That's not true” or “the service never stopped.” On a thin file that sentence is contradicted by the file you are about to show, and the client stops believing the rest.

4 · Ask, don't warn

Do: “Has anyone been in touch with you about moving your policy?” Whatever the answer, thank them and do not react.
Don't: “Be careful, someone may call you.” A warning tells the client we are worried about ourselves.

5 · Give the law as a right, never as a threat — and say “today”, not “still”

Do: “Under the Insurance Act, anyone who suggests you replace this has to set out the advantages and the disadvantages for you first. Ask for it in writing.” And: “Ask anyone who advises you which company their certificate names today — and ask me the same.”
Don't: quote fines or prison terms, say that anyone is breaking the law, or ask whether someone is “still” registered. “Still” points at someone; “today” is a question about everyone, including you.

6 · Concede the true part first

Do: “That's true, and I'll say it myself: I can only offer Guardian Life. Which is why I'm not asking you to buy anything — keeping what you have costs nothing and changes nothing.”
Don't: deflect or deny a point that is simply true — the branch is tied to one insurer; a broker is a fair thing to consider; “orphan policy” is real trade usage. Agreeing with the true part of a pitch is what makes the untrue part visible.

7 · Never count

Do: silence on how many left, when, or why. If pressed: “I won't speak to that. I can speak to your policy.”
Don't: “only a few”, “not that many”, “most of us stayed”. Any number, even a smaller one, concedes that the number is the question.

8 · Offer nothing to stay

Do: “Your policy is unaffected. There are two ways to pick this up — which would you rather?” If a client has been offered a gift or a free month elsewhere: “The only premium that exists is the one written on the policy — for any company, including us.”
Don't: a discount, a visit as a favour, a promise the branch has not made in writing. It would be matched, it teaches the client to shop, and under the Act a rebate is unlawful for everyone.

9 · Health is a form, never a compliment

Do: “The good news is you've already been through underwriting — this policy is issued, and nothing about your health today can touch it.”
Don't: guess whether the client would pass underwriting again, or say what another company would or would not offer them. You do not know, and the client will remember the guess.

10 · Numbers in writing, never from memory

Do: fetch the paid-to date, the surrender value, the paid-up value and the premium at today's age, and put them in front of the client in writing. “Let me get you three numbers, then you'll know exactly what you're comparing.”
Don't: quote a figure on the call, or say “cover always costs more when you're older”. Rate tables change; the only safe claim is the one the written comparison shows.

11 · Promise only what Guardian Life has confirmed

Do: “Anything on this policy is done through Guardian Life, and I can do it with you.” If the client asks to change who looks after the policy: “Ask the branch — it never needs the policy ended.”
Don't: “It's just a note on your file, I'll switch it now” until the branch has confirmed with Guardian Life what it records as the servicing intermediary on an in-force policy. A promise the branch cannot keep is worse than the pitch.

12 · Be the person who answers, from this minute

Do: your own number and the branch number, a day and a time you will call back, and then the call at that time. On an outbound call: “You have a name now — mine — and you didn't have to click anything to get it.” Lead every later contact with something new: the paid-up value they were never told, the beneficiary that needs confirming, the conversion option before a term runs out.
Don't: “someone will be in touch”, “the promise has been kept” to a client who never asked, or “just checking in”. The first is what an orphan policy sounds like; the last is the call that gets declined next time.

13 · Leave them in control

Do: “Whatever you decide, we'll ask again rather than assume.”
Don't: press for a decision on the call. The review filed next week beats the promise extracted today.
